On Prefigurative Writing

I recently came across an introductory piece on prefigurative politics that moves between the Zapatistas, Rojava, and the MST, allotting each case under a hundred words, closing each section with a quotation from Subcomandante Marcos or bell hooks or Buckminster Fuller. It claims to introduce a politics of “means are ends.” Halfway through I realised the piece was arriving at prefigurative ends through anti-prefigurative means, and was wholly unaware of the contradiction.

If means are ends, then writing about prefiguration must itself be prefigurative. Otherwise you are using a form that betrays your politics to advertise your politics. The problem is epistemological, and it has several identifiable modes of failure.

The first: quotations carrying emotional weight rather than argumentative weight. Bell hooks wrote “what we cannot imagine cannot come into being” inside a specific lineage of Black feminist reclamation of imagination as political resource. Its function is to make the paragraph feel heavier than it actually is. Prefiguration demands that form carry its own political work; outsourcing the liberation of imagination to hooks’s name severs means from ends. Borneman’s claim that care is the source and result of human creativity holds because he spent an entire essay on the relation between Dieter and Harald, a bond unaccommodated by any kinship category, doing the work of holding it up (1997, p. 583). A quotation that does not carry its original argumentative weight is only ornament.

The second and more concealed failure: flattening different situated knowledges into homogenous inspiration. The Zapatistas’ political architecture grew from a specific suturing of Mayan cosmology and Marxist tradition; Rojava’s democratic confederalism arrived through Bookchin via Öcalan’s particular reading; the MST is embedded in the legal history of Brazilian land reform and the soil of liberation theology. Real tensions exist between the three on questions of sovereignty, scale, and relation to the state. Lining them up as “sources of inspiration,” each allotted equal word count and equal rhetorical weight, is itself an epistemic flattening. The whole stake of Haraway’s situated knowledge is that objectivity is the honest marking of a position; flattening into interchangeable case studies performs its inverse. Munhazim and Zondon, writing on queer Muslim death, insist that “living deaths” and “unlived desires” cannot be absorbed into a universal rhetoric of surviving (2021), because absorption converts structurally silenced dying into consumable “inspiration.” Yet movement-tourism writing does exactly this.

The third and most awkward failure: writing in the future tense about building that is happening now. Such pieces keep saying “we must imagine,” “we should construct”, while actual prefigurative practice has already been building. Dakar’s waste pickers are already running an alternative economy and Mbeubeuss has its own territorial divisions and governance structures. Palestinian families in Sheikh Jarrah stay daily through legal struggle and bodily presence. Packaging this ongoing building as “a future we can dream of” is a violence of tense, it demotes present-tense political work into future-tense inspirational narrative and sells the demotion as hope. Means are ends means the future is here and rewriting building as imagining pushes it back into the future.

What does prefigurative writing look like, then? Allen’s There’s a Disco Ball Between Us is a clear example. Allen writes Black gay time space by moving between the moment Kevin Aviance saw his dead girlfriends in the Palladium and the metaphor of the Atlantic as a collection of mirrors, placing the reader inside the fragmented light the disco ball throws off. The form does the political work the book claims, anti-linear ends cannot be reached by linear means, so Allen did not write linearly. The Munhazim and Zondon collaborative autoethnography works the same way: it refuses to draw a clean line between the analyst’s position and the lives being analysed, because that line is itself the epistemological violence they are protesting.

To clarify, an analytical piece, comparing how Zapatistas and Rojava conceptualise sovereignty, can perfectly well take a conventional academic form, because its ends are analytical understanding instead of prefiguration. The problem only arises when writing claims to do prefigurative political work, when the ends are sparking prefigurative practice while the means are compressing radical politics into broth, flattening situated struggle into homogenous inspiration, rewriting present-tense building as future-tense dreaming. It is in those moments, the failure of form is the failure of politics.

The argument is also a constraint on my own writing. I keep returning, in the “slopes of care” series, because if I do not let their specificity resist my generalisation, for example if I let Rukban become a case study about dereferencing rather than Rukban itself, I am performing the violence I have just described. The minimum requirement of means are ends, applied to writing, is that: do not use a form that renders its object consumable to write about conditions that are not consumable.
