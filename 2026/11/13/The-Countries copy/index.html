Can an Algorithm Ever Be Decolonial?

In my first year at LSE I attended a public lecture by Austin Zeiderman on Artery, his book about Colombia’s Magdalena River, and someone in the audience asked whether infrastructure could ever be decolonial. Zeiderman answered that his work was analytic and did not set out to prescribe what should be done, and in a post I wrote afterwards I argued that this was a retreat. A year and a half later I find myself facing the same question about a different kind of infrastructure. Police forces and courts now use algorithms to predict crime and to assess who is likely to reoffend, and people usually assume a model without ‘race’ as a category cannot discriminate by race. I want to ask whether such a model could ever be decolonial.

The evidence so far suggests that race-blind models fail. When Lum and Isaac ran a simulation of the PredPol algorithm on Oakland’s drug arrest records in 2016, it would have sent police to Black neighbourhoods at roughly twice the rate of white ones, although survey estimates suggested that drug use was spread fairly evenly across the city, because the model learned from records of where police had chosen to patrol. In the COMPAS case, ProPublica found that among defendants in Broward County who did not reoffend, 44.9 percent of Black defendants had been labelled higher risk against 23.5 percent of white defendants, and later work by Chouldechova and by Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan proved that when groups differ in their rates of rearrest no score can be fair in every sense at once, so some group must carry more of the errors. These findings are usually seen as engineering problems, and people tend to hope for a better model trained on cleaner data and built with fairer constraints.

I do not think the better model would help, and the reason is fairly obvious. Even a model free of these problems would still sort people into those it marks as risky, and the people it marks would come to be treated the way racialised groups have been treated. The Metropolitan Police’s Gangs Violence Matrix, for instance: Amnesty found in 2018 that around a third of those listed had never been convicted of a serious offence, while the listing still followed them into decisions about housing and immigration. Thus, the more important question is why a society keeps producing such groups whatever tools it uses.

Gilmore defines racism as the production of group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death. I read the definition as saying that the decision comes before the group. A society that distributes exposure to harm unequally, through policing, housing, healthcare and work, needs a way of making that distribution look natural, and racialisation is the means by which it does so. A London officer quoted in one of our GY315 lectures, who said that stopping cars full of young Black men was simply a matter of fact and had nothing to do with racism, shows how this works at the level of a single judgement, because the unequal treatment is presented as a description of the world. A risk score does the same thing, because it has no author who can be challenged and it arrives in the form of “rational”/“neutral” arithmetic. The algorithm thus sits downstream of the problem, the unequal distribution of exposure exists first and the algorithm is just one more instrument for carrying it out.

As long as a society allocates who may be exposed to harm, some form of racialisation will do the work of making that allocation acceptable. An algorithm therefore cannot be decolonial, because decoloniality belongs to the social arrangement that the algorithm serves, and no adjustment to its inputs changes that arrangement.

Pessimism of this kind is risky, as it is tempting to conclude that nothing can be done and that the honest scholar should only describe the problem, and that conclusion would turn my argument into another analytic retreat. Bell faced the same difficulty when he argued in 1992 that racism is a permanent feature of American society, and his response was that the struggle against it remains necessary and has value in itself, even if final victory never comes. Gilmore describes abolition as the building of institutions that make people less vulnerable as much as the closing of prisons. On this account the work of decolonisation lies in reducing the allocation of exposure itself through collective provision such as housing and healthcare.

My answer to the question Zeiderman was asked, applied to algorithms, is therefore no, and I suspect that no infrastructure can be decolonial on its own. However, saying no is different from saying that nothing can be done. The effort now spent on building fairer machines would do more good if it went into reducing the need to sort people in the first place.
