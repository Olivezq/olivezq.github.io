Filth Commons

I have lost, in one way or another, the vitality to write.

In the gaps between stations, not a single moving sentence came to me. I stood watching for a long while as children ran in the distance, tripping over each other, and a golden retriever bounced back and forth like an enchanted scarecrow taking flight. A seagull swept past like a warship crossing a grassland as vast as a sixth ocean.

It suddenly occurred to me that I don’t like using personal pronouns. Because to be “in the Filth Commons” means we no longer meet the administrative definition of “the livings.” We are objects outside the subject-body of domestic residents, of one substance with waste. Of one substance with the seagull, of one substance with the grassland. Identity is fluid or has been stripped away, so our sentences usually carry no first-person subject. We are together. I am nothing more than one eye in the body that is the Filth Commons; the seagull is a little finger; the grassland is a patch of skin.

My physical death is only a temporary blindness of ours. The seagull’s death is only a brief spasm in a little finger. Each fulfilling a role while alive, none knowing the other, yet all housed within the same body. We constitute the body, but the body sustains no great loss when it loses us.

Outside last night’s guesthouse stood a mask, resembling the classic demon figures from home, yet belonging to some distant religious ritual. The host’s skin was a dark green, faintly exuding the smell of chemicals. A diffuser sat in the room; I couldn’t sleep. The door had no keyhole. At any moment a silhouette might appear against the translucent window. Another set of blinds kept flaring up suddenly, disturbed by the motion-sensor light as pedestrians passed. At dinnertime, that child cried a lot. The hostress had her heavy back turned to me as I left. I wandered through the darkened streets, only a cat darting past the stalled cars. Then back again, to this house at the end of the road.

This morning I finally caught sight of the hostress. She returned my greeting with a worn-out look and hurried past. Will my future be the same, eking out an existence in some corner of the Filth Commons? Forced to rent out part of the house as a cheap guesthouse, strangers endlessly entering the private home, the child always crying. Yet the home itself always frightfully quiet. Oh, home comes from far away. They say some marginalized groups have already “erupted in silence,” but we cannot feel it. Love and violence alike arrive slowly.
