What Does It Mean to Be ‘Middle Class’?

The reflections here emerged from a recent lunchtime discussion with Prof. Mercer on middle-class formation in African cities, where I had the opportunity to raise questions about how we understand class across different cultural contexts.

The term “middle class” is ubiquitous in development discourse. It appears in policy briefs and newspaper headlines celebrating the rise of new consumer markets in Asia and Africa. However this seemingly straightforward category conceals an epistemological problem. What happens when the people we label as “middle class” do not recognise themselves in that term? And what does it mean for our analytical frameworks when this category is actively unspoken?

During a discussion on Tanzania, I learned that people do not use the term “middle class” in everyday conversation. There is no commonly deployed Swahili equivalent, one can translate it, of course, but the phrase does not circulate in the way it might in Ghana, India, or the United Kingdom. This absence is deeply rooted in Tanzania’s socialist history, specifically the ujamaa period under Julius Nyerere, during which the middle classes were ideologically positioned as enemies of the nation, parasites living off the labour of rural farmers, who were celebrated as the authentic owners of the Tanzanian state. Even today, unlike some African nations that proudly tout the size of their emerging middle class as evidence of developmental success, the Tanzanian state remains largely silent on the matter. The middle class exists but it exists without a name.

This raises a methodological challenge that I find compelling. If the people we study do not call themselves middle class, on what grounds do we, as researchers, impose that label? The response I encountered was twofold. First, one does not need to consciously name oneself as a class to be a class. Class can be performed through material practices, through the act of building a particular kind of house on the urban periphery, for instance, which signals a specific social position regardless of whether its owner articulates it in those terms. Second, the absence of class discourse is itself historically and politically significant. The silence is a product of specific ideological formations that made certain ways of speaking about society possible while foreclosing others. In this sense, the question is rather “what are the historical conditions under which class becomes speakable or unspeakable?”

This insight connects to broader debates in feminist and postcolonial scholarship about the politics of knowledge production. Just as feminist scholars like Donna Haraway have argued that all knowledge is situated and partial, so too must we recognise that analytical categories like “middle class” carry their own genealogies and assumptions. When we apply such categories to contexts where they do not emerge organically from local discourse, we risk imposing frameworks that obscure as much as they reveal. Yet abandoning the category entirely would mean losing a powerful tool for understanding how social stratification operates and reproduces itself. The challenge, then, is to hold both truths simultaneously, to remain attentive to the ways “class” travels and sometimes fails to translate.

What I found worth exploring is about the observation that most studies of middle-class formation capture a particular historical moment but rarely return to examine how things have changed. Leela Fernandes’s India’s New Middle Class (2006), for example, was published at a specific conjuncture, when India was embracing economic liberalisation and opening its markets to global capital. The book documented the emergence of a new consuming middle class at that precise moment. However nearly two decades later, no systematic restudy has traced how India’s middle class has evolved, how its interests have intersected with the rise of Hindutva nationalism, or how its composition has shifted with India’s changing role in the global economy. This is a potential direction for research about the temporal dimension of class formation.

There was also a fascinating observation about the instability of class markers. The material practices that once signalled middle-class status are constantly “trickling down” as consumer goods become more widely accessible. Take weddings as an example. Over the past three decades, wedding practices in Tanzania have transformed, tiered cakes, decorated halls, sparkly lights, and globalised music have become aspirational markers that can be partially replicated regardless of income level. One does not need the complete package; one can have something that gestures toward it, drawing ideas from Instagram and TikTok. The same applies to building materials, architectural flourishes, and household goods. Yet, crucially, having a fancy wedding cake does not make one middle class. The markers of distinction keep shifting, because they keep being appropriated. This creates an endless race for differentiation, a phenomenon that Pierre Bourdieu would recognise as the constant repositioning of social actors within a field of cultural capital.

Income-based approaches, while valuable for tracking changes over time, struggle to capture the relational and performative dimensions of class. In contexts where people have multiple income streams, such as a small grocery business or agricultural investments in rural areas, asking for a “monthly income” produces data that may be technically accurate but socially meaningless. Housing, by contrast, offers a material trace of class position that is literally visible and socially legible. The house speaks even when its owner does not. It also signals accumulation and social location in ways that income figures cannot.

This methodological point connects to a broader argument about how we might approach the study of class in the Global South. Rather than beginning with a predetermined category and searching for its local instantiation, we might instead begin with material practices. Moreover, the most productive research often comes not from confirming what we already suspect but from being genuinely surprised by what we find.

For those interested in pursuing research in this area, several directions seem promising. One approach would be to conduct a “restudy”, to return to a site where middle-class formation was documented fifteen or twenty years ago and ask what has changed. How do the dynamics of class-making at one historical conjuncture differ from those at another? What continuities persist beneath apparent transformations? Another direction would examine the politics of class discourse itself: why do some contexts produce active public discussion about the middle class while others suppress it? What are the political conditions under which class becomes visible or invisible as a category of public speech? A third possibility would trace the “trickle-down” of class markers over time, examining how specific material practices move through social strata and what new forms of distinction emerge to replace those that have been democratised.

This lunchtime reminded me that the categories we use to understand the social world are themselves products of that world. In Tanzania, the middle class builds houses, accumulates property, and reproduces itself through specific spatial practices, all without ever naming itself as such. Understanding why this is the case, and what it means for how we study class more broadly, would be questions worth pursuing.
