Can Natural Capital Be Replaced? Notes from Eric Neumayer's Lecture

On 10 February, the LSE Department of Geography and Environment, together with the Grantham Research Institute on Climate Change and the Environment and the LSE Sustainability Initiative, hosted a public lecture by Professor Eric Neumayer to mark the open-access publication of the fifth edition of his book, Weak versus Strong Sustainability, first published in 1999. The event was held at the Shaw Theatre and chaired by Professor Hyun Bang Shin. What follows is a summary of the lecture, with connections drawn to the core concepts covered in GY222 (Applied Environmental Economics).

Every 90 minutes, around seven million tonnes of CO₂ are emitted, approximately 1,000 football pitches of primary tropical rainforest disappear, somewhere between one and seven species go extinct, and roughly 1,400 people die from air pollution-related illness. These figures, Prof. Neumayer argued, frame the central question of his life’s work: does any of this actually matter, from an economic standpoint? The answer turns out depending entirely on which theory of sustainability one adopts.

The I=PAT Framework and What It Misses

Students of GY222 will recognise the I=PAT identity (Impact = Population × Affluence × Technology) as a diagnostic tool for breaking down the sources of environmental pressure. Neumayer’s lecture implicitly engages with the “A” and “T” terms throughout: the question of whether increasing affluence (A) necessarily generates environmental degradation, and whether technological change (T) can decouple that relationship.

This is the terrain of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC), another concept central to GY222. The EKC hypothesis, associated with Grossman and Krueger’s 1995 work, suggests an inverted-U relationship between income per capita and pollution. Degradation increases in early stages of development and eventually declines as countries grow wealthier. The lecture engages with this optimism directly. For some pollutants, the EKC story holds up. For carbon emissions and municipal solid waste, it notably does not. And for tropical deforestation, the trend line is not an inverted U but a one-way street moving relentlessly upward.

The Capital Framework

The conceptual heart of the lecture, and of Neumayer’s book, is a framework students already encounter in GY222, about the distinction between different forms of capital and their role in sustaining welfare across generations.

Drawing on Robert Solow’s economic definition of sustainability (leaving future generations “the capacity to be as well off as we are today”), the lecture sets up the central question in terms of a production function f(K, R), where K is physical capital and R is natural capital. The key analytical question is: what happens when R approaches zero? Can more K compensate? Students familiar with the GY222 treatment of factors of production (physical capital, labour and human capital, natural resources, and technology), can immediately recognise that this is the substitutability debate rendered formally.

Weak sustainability assumes K and R are substitutes. The total capital stock must be non-declining over time, but its composition can shift freely. On this view, depleting an oil field is acceptable so long as equivalent value is reinvested in, say, education or infrastructure. The mathematical implication is that consumption C must not decrease over time: C_t = C_{t+1}.

Strong sustainability treats K and R as complements rather than substitutes. In a Leontief-type production function (Y = K × R), if R = 0, then Y = 0 regardless of how much K is accumulated. Natural capital stock S must itself be maintained: S_t = S_{t+1}. The existing stock cannot be duplicated by manufactured capital because natural systems perform functions that no factory can replicate.

Two Paradigms

Where does the evidence land? On non-renewable resources, weak sustainability largely wins. Contrary to the “Limits to Growth” predictions of the early 1970s, which assumed no substitution and no technological change, there is no near-term risk of running out of oil, gas, or metals. The problem with fossil fuels is about climate, that there is far too much carbon in the grounde. Students studying the Limits to Growth debate in GY222 will find Neumayer’s response useful here: the dire predictions of the MIT model depended critically on its assumptions about substitutability and technological change, precisely the parameters the weak/strong sustainability debate contests.

On renewable resources and pollution, however, strong sustainability is more persuasive. Tropical primary forest loss is accelerating, with 2024 among the worst years on record. Scientists estimate that deforestation of 20–25% of the Amazon may be sufficient to trigger an irreversible tipping point, a shift to savanna-type vegetation, yet current deforestation sits at 14–17% and is rising. For carbon emissions, global temperatures are already on track to exceed 1.5°C of warming, with Neumayer predicting 3–3.5°C as a realistic outcome once positive feedback loops (including permafrost methane release) are accounted for.

Norway vs. the UK

A key practical implication of weak sustainability is that resource extraction rents must be invested. This distinction matters enormously. Consuming the proceeds of natural resource depletion is not sustainable even under weak sustainability assumptions; what matters is whether extraction revenues are channelled into other forms of capital that can sustain future welfare.

The Norway/UK comparison is instructive. Both countries extracted North Sea oil. Norway channelled revenues into a sovereign wealth fund, now exceeding $1 trillion in value. The UK did not adopt a comparable mechanism. From a weak sustainability standpoint, Norway’s approach is at least directionally correct; the UK’s consumption of its oil windfall is much harder to defend. This example is a useful illustration for exam purposes of what “investing rents” looks like in practice, and what failure to do so looks like.

The Discount Rate Problem and the Limits of CBA

One of the most analytically challenging sections of the lecture concerned the role of the discount rate in climate policy. The core issue is a temporal asymmetry: the costs of emission reduction fall on the present, while the benefits accrue far in the future. A high discount rate (consistent with current market rates of 4–5%) makes aggressive climate action appear unjustified in a standard cost-benefit framework. A low discount rate, as favoured by Nicholas Stern, makes it appear urgently necessary.

Neumayer’s contribution here is to argue that this framing may be the wrong one altogether. When the damages at stake include irreversible losses of non-substitutable natural capital the cost-benefit framework breaks down. It is of recognising that some things cannot be adequately captured in monetary present-value terms at all.

ESG, SDGs, and the Weak Sustainability Default

The lecture also touched on a question directly relevant to how sustainability is measured and governed in practice. ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) frameworks, Neumayer suggested, are essentially weak sustainability instruments. By aggregating heterogeneous indicators into composite scores, they obscure the distinction between capital types that can be traded off and those that cannot. The charge of greenwashing may be too blunt, but ESG as currently designed does not distinguish between substitutable and non-substitutable natural capital.

This connects to a broader theme in GY222: the three-pillar model of sustainable development (economic, social, environmental) and the Brundtland definition are both largely normative frameworks that leave unanswered the analytical question of substitutability. The SDGs similarly represent a weak sustainability orientation, treating all 17 goals as fungible priorities rather than identifying any as lexicographically prior.

Conclusion

Several angles:

The weak/strong sustainability debate maps onto real policy disagreements (Paris Agreement vs. binding emissions targets; EKC optimism vs. tipping point concerns). Neumayer’s point that neither paradigm is strictly falsifiable is analytically important: the debate is about making a persuasive case under uncertainty.

The production function formulation (f(K, R), substitutes vs. complements) provides a clean way to formalise the intuition behind weak and strong sustainability in economic terms, useful for any question on capital theory or intergenerational equity.

The discount rate debate (Stern vs. Nordhaus) is well-trodden ground, but Neumayer adds a deeper layer that even getting the discount rate right may not be sufficient if the losses involved are irreversible and non-substitutable. Cost-benefit analysis also has limits as a methodology.

The Norway case provides a concrete, empirically grounded example of what weak sustainability requires in practice and illustrates the political economy reasons why it so often fails.

Finally, the question of who bears the costs of transition, and who caused the problem in the first place, connects the weak/strong sustainability debate to questions of climate justice and North-South inequality that run through GY222.
