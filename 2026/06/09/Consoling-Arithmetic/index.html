Consoling Arithmetic

“It is consoling that the injustice of the world seems to be merely a matter of arithmetic.” —- Kafka

There is usually a common move in critical writing: it spends a long stretch analyzing a structural, political problem, lays out its historical and geographical conditions, and then in the conclusion, relocates the solution to a different level — individual cognition, say, or school curricula. This level has two features: actionable, available to be written up as concrete recommendations; does not touch the structure the analysis has just examined. I will call this move displacement, and nearly all writing of this kind ends this way.

For instances: Kimura’s gendered scientization: a political question about the distribution of power is recast as a technical one, handed to experts to adjudicate, so that the structure producing the problem is no longer held to account. The deficit model: it assumes the public fails to act because it does not know enough, so the problem becomes one of communication, make the science clear enough and action will follow; its real effect is to shift responsibility from government onto media and education, which is the same step as scientization. Decolonial scholarship: having analyzed land and sovereignty, it almost always concludes that schools should add more decolonial curriculum. The three are one move, differing only in where they land, and the decolonial case is the clearest because what gets analyzed is a historically sedimented arrangement of power, while what gets prescribed is a change in individual cognition. Curriculum becomes the default answer since it satisfies two conditions at once: it can be written as an “action recommendation,” and it will not actually alter the structure exposed.

Haraway has a line, that objects should be figured as actors and agents, not as screens, grounds, or resources. Applied to the deficit model: the way it regards “the public” is the same as the way extractive development regards land and indigenous people, both treat the other as a background or a fillable resource rather than as an agent with a standpoint of its own. So the remedy on both sides is the same: instead of speaking more loudly, we should recognize that the other was an actor all along. The difference between the Guardian and the BBC can be read against this criterion, though the conclusion is not simple. The Guardian, reporting on the environment, lets indigenous communities speak as subjects; the BBC, in the name of neutrality, piles on hedges and leaves the same communities in the background. By the actor/agent criterion the Guardian does better. But the BBC is not being lazy as well; it is just obeying a different criterion, neutrality and comprehensiveness. This shows that figuring an object as an actor is not free, as it requires choosing a standpoint, and choosing a standpoint sacrifices neutrality. The two criteria conflict here, so one cannot simply say everyone should follow the Guardian (or BBC).

Displacement and de-agentification look opposite: one assigns too much agency to the individual (you should learn more, schools should teach more), the other denies the object any agency at all. They are in fact two ends of a single problem, and what matters is not the amount of agency but where it is placed. Scientization and the deficit model place agency in the isolated individual; extractive development places it in “the one who decides.” Both assume agency is a property of discrete units that can be counted off and summed, and to sum them, one must first take the units as commensurable, as homogeneous and differing only in quantity; both therefore deny another possibility that agency is distributed across relations, and that the relation precedes each of the individuals within it. So Haraway’s line is an ontological claim, which the deficit model and coloniality both refuse.

This distinction handles two places that were otherwise stuck. First, “the land should be no one’s to decide” sounds right but turns out unusable, because whoever says it still treats the land as an object to be decided about; if the land is an actor, then “protect it or develop it” are two framings that both treat the land as a resource, and the difference lies not in which you choose but in the position of “being decided about” itself, which makes it the same kind of problem as the deficit model rather than an impasse with no way through. Second, the affective turn. Studies show that more knowledge does not produce more action, while people with a kinship or religious connection to nature are more willing to protect it; for example climate melancholia: grief over the environment drives protection. But affect does not automatically avoid displacement, if kinship means “the individual should feel a little more,” it is only the deficit model with facts swapped for feelings, agency still lodged in the isolated individual; only when kinship means “the human was always already inside a web of relations shared with non-human actors” does it land on the side of relation. The criterion throughout is where agency is placed, not how strong the feeling is.

The judgment now has to be turned on this writing itself. If I say “the problem is that agency has been put in the wrong place, and we should adopt a relational ontology,” then I am performing the very move described at the outset: a political problem about land and the distribution of power ends up relocated onto “switching to another ontology.” This landing point is even safer than curriculum, since curriculum at least has to fight for teaching hours and funding, whereas a change of ontology demands nothing and disturbs no existing arrangement. “We need a relational worldview” may be the easiest to say and the least threatening kind of displacement there is. This cannot be avoided by more careful phrasing, that critique must land somewhere, and any landing point is easier to reach than the structure it criticizes.

The Guardian’s rewriting of David Attenborough — pointing out that he is not only an environmental public figure but also an advocate of radical economic redistribution — made a flattened image complex again. That is complete in itself because it does not need “did it change anyone’s behavior” to confirm it. In Austin’s terms, this is the illocutionary effect; action belongs to the perlocutionary, a contingent aftereffect. The deficit model’s error is to fuse the two, assuming that informing necessarily yields behaviour change. That knowledge does not bring action while kinship does is the counterexample to this fusion. Separating the two lets one say both that the deficit model’s efficacy assumption is arbitrary and that the article nonetheless has value.

But this distinction also has its own politics: the media can use it to deflect that “our job is to make things clear; action is someone else’s business”, which is again a transfer of responsibility. So whoever uses it must also admit it can be used this way.

And there is a more awkward problem. Can an illocutionary effect stand on its own, apart from reception? If making an image complex is received by no one, and if most people now no longer know who Attenborough is, then was anything accomplished at all? This shows that the illocutionary too quietly assumes a condition of reception, that is, a web of relations. In other words, even this seemingly clean distinction cannot escape the relational premise set out earlier: production counts only if there is consumption.

Back to Kafka’s line. “Arithmetic” here carries two senses. The first is the consoling one: if injustice is a ledger, then position is contingent, you were not singled out by some essence or fate, you just ame up at this number; discrimination, accordingly, is not the essence of any category but a quantity assigned by position, addable and subtractable, and an account that sums this way can be re-summed that way, so no one stays on top forever. The second is the violent one: to write a person into an account is to treat them as a countable, replaceable unit, and the living dead (those who exist only as quantity and can therefore be both managed and discarded) are therefore produced.

These are two ends of one operation, joined by the commensurability of units: only once people have been reduced to homogeneous units differing solely in quantity can addition and comparison take place at all (sociology calls this process commensuration, after Espeland and Stevens). Commensurability is what the earlier methodological individualism looks like at the level of the unit, to treat value or agency as a sum of parts, one must first delete from each part whatever is non-substitutable and cannot be entered as a quantity. So the consolation of the first sense and the violence of the second buy the same thing that position can be re-summed only because the person has first been made into a re-summable unit. Consolation is capture because being consoled is itself consent to that commensuration.

The opposite logic is the line, if one being is not delivered, no being is delivered. Its point is not sameness in the sense of “equality”, that “sameness” is just commensurability, everyone the same unit. Its point is incommensurability, deliverance cannot be broken into separate deliverances summed together, and no scale lets one person’s being-delivered offset another’s not-being-delivered, so salvation is either whole or it does not hold. The equality of all beings here is not that each is an identical share but that no one is a share. This is what a relational ontology says in an older language, that a relation is not a sum of units.

Finally, back to the conflict I set aside. To write an object as an actor, one must choose a standpoint; to be comprehensive and neutral, one must suspend it. The two requirements pull against each other, with the BBC’s hedges and the Guardian’s partiality each holding one end. How many studies or reports manage both? The question should not be answered with a tidy synthesis, because it is the cost of putting a relational ontology into practice. Once you grant something a standpoint, it becomes hard to stay neutral. Thus, I will not offer a solution here, because by the logic above, any “solution” too easily becomes one more displacement.
