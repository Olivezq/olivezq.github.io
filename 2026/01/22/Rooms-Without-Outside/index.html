January 22, 2026

Rooms Without Outside

“Everything is possible, yet nothing is possible. Everything is permitted, yet nothing is permitted… Everything has an explanation, yet nothing has an explanation. Nothing is more valuable than anything else, and no idea is better than any other.” — E.M. Cioran, On the Heights of Despair

Prologue

September 2020, Changwon, South Korea. Police forced open the door of a small rented room. The handle had been tied shut from inside with rope. On the floor lay the decomposing bodies of a mother, 51, and her daughter, 22.

The walls were covered in hand-drawn pictures. The mother’s were colourful: symmetrical figures, two people facing each other, fashionably dressed, smiling. The daughter’s were black and white: anatomical diagrams, flowcharts of life and death, organs methodically labelled.

No one knows what happened inside. The coroner could not determine the cause of death and neighbours said they hadn’t seen either woman in months.

Psychiatry has a term for this: Shared Delusional Disorder, or Folie à deux. When one person’s delusional belief is adopted by someone in close relationship, the two construct a closed interpretive system together. Inside this system, everything is coherent: the outside world is dangerous, only each other can be trusted, and any attempt at intervention proves the threat is real.

A similar case occurred in the UK a few years ago. A Japanese family, three children, all graduates of elite universities lived together in complete isolation. When the eldest daughter died, the family wiped her body with alcohol, believing she was still alive. When police finally entered, the mother insisted that “She’s just sleeping.”

But this post is not about psychiatric pathology. I am interested in a more fundamental question: What happens when an interpretive system becomes completely closed, when it no longer allows any external input to alter itself? And more sharply: How do we determine who is deluded? On what grounds can science claim it is not simply another closed system?

When Judgement Loses Its Outside

Cioran’s passage is often read as nihilism. But looking again, he is not describing the absence but its excess of meaning.

“Everything has an explanation, yet nothing has an explanation.” This is a world with too much meaning. When all paths are permitted, no path leads anywhere. When everything can be explained, explanation loses its power to distinguish.

This is the condition of contemporary information environments. Online, any event generates countless explanations, from conspiracy theories to official narratives. Each has its own evidence chain and forms a system. The problem is thus that we have too much information and no shared criteria for judgement.

In such a world, “trust the science” is not an epistemological position. It is political, one that presumes an answer to the question it should be asking, that, who has the authority to define what counts as science?

Return to that room in Changwon. Psychiatry would diagnose the mother and daughter with Shared Delusional Disorder. This suggests their beliefs were “wrong.” However what makes a belief “delusional” rather than “conviction” or “theory”?

The traditional answer is that delusion is belief that contradicts reality. But this definition is circular. Who defines reality? By what standard?

I propose a different definition, that, delusion is belief that has lost its refutability.

In Changwon, the mother believed the outside world was dangerous. This belief is not inherently absurd as danger exists. The problem is that no evidence could disprove it. When neighbours knocked, it confirmed the threat. When the daughter wanted to go out, it showed she had been “contaminated.” Every potential counter-evidence was absorbed in advance as confirmation.

This is how closed systems operate, by having explanations so complete that nothing can enter from outside.

Thomas Kuhn argued that science does not progress linearly. It moves through cycles, from normal science, to crisis, to revolution, to new normal science.

During “normal science,” scientists work within a shared paradigm. They accept certain assumptions, use them to define valid questions and methods. In this sense, normal science resembles delusion as both depend on shared premises and maintain internal consistency.

But Kuhn identified a crucial difference, as in science, anomalies are allowed to accumulate. When enough phenomena resist explanation, the scientific community enters “crisis”, often with confusion and uncertainty. This crisis can eventually produce “paradigm shift” where a new set of assumptions replaces the old. And this is what delusion lacks. In that Changwon room, every discordant signal was immediately digested into the existing framework. No crisis was possible, therefore no revolution was possible. The system remained stable, until physical death ended everything.

Bruno Latour offered another perspective. Scientific facts are “solid” because they are supported by vast networks such as laboratories, peer review, funding bodies, and public trust. When any node in this network breaks, “facts” become unstable.

Latour is often misread as saying “science is fiction.” What he actually meant was that scientific objectivity is not given but achieved. Science is fragile. Its openness and capacity for self-correction all depend on specific social institutions and cultural conditions.

What happens when those conditions erode?

During COVID-19, “Follow the science” became a political slogan. The intention was good as in an environment flooded with misinformation, emphasise that expert judgement deserves respect. But the slogan contains a paradox.

Science deserves trust is because it does not demand unconditional trust. Its power comes from fallibility, meaning that any theory can be overturned by new evidence. As “trust the science” becomes a moral command, it betrays the spirit of science itself. More dangerously, when “science” is used to terminate discussion, it begins to exhibit the same structural features as delusion: irrefutable and intolerant of anomaly.

Argentine sociologist Adrián Scribano proposed, in his “sociology of hope,” a concept that seems unrelated but is in fact crucial.

Scribano argued that hope is not a psychological state or emotion, but a social structure. Specifically, hope is the experience that “the world has not yet been fully determined”, the capacity to remain open to the future.

Translated into epistemological terms: Hope = the possibility that interpretation can still be negotiated and rearranged.

Hope is the condition under which judgement systems remain open. When hope exists, we acknowledge our understanding is provisional to the future. When hope disappears, the world is “closed off” in advance as all answers already written. In this sense, delusion can be redefined as: belief that has lost the structure of hope. Becoming belief that no longer permits change.

The Changwon mother and daughter lost hope in the epistemological sense, of the possibility that the world might be different from what they believed. They chose a world that no longer changes, and death was merely the physical consequence.

The Second Room: Institutional Closure

Now let us enter another room.

England, 2024. Approximately 159,000 children live in “temporary accommodation”, hotels, hostels, converted shipping containers. The word “temporary” is misleading as average stays stretch into years, and some children spend their entire childhood in these spaces.

These rooms have no ropes tying door handles shut. But they are closed in a different way.

The housing crisis in England is not simply a shortage of homes. It is a system that produces and reproduces homelessness through interconnected policy mechanisms: Right to Buy depleted social housing stock (2 million units lost since 1980); replacement building never caught up; private rents rose; Local Housing Allowance was frozen; local councils, legally obligated to house children but chronically underfunded, turned to expensive temporary accommodation.

This is what journalist Dan Hewitt calls the “doom loop.” And the system is functioning exactly as designed.

Here is where the parallel with delusion becomes precise. Research by the Debt Trap Nation project reveals that 88% of English local authorities have housing debt disqualification rules. If a family owes rent arrears, often incurred because of housing instability, they can be barred from social housing waiting lists or have their priority reduced. In December 2023 alone, nearly 4,000 households were banned from bidding on social housing due to debt. 43% contained children. 5% had infants under two years old.

The debt is real but so was the structural violence that produced it. A mother fleeing domestic abuse may leave with nothing but debt. The years in temporary accommodation have no kitchen, so takeaway costs rise; children’s schools change repeatedly, so transport costs accumulate. The debt then becomes grounds for exclusion from stable housing, which generates more instability, which generates more debt.

This is a closed loop. But unlike the Changwon room, it is sealed by policy language. Every decision follows protocol and yet the cumulative effect is indistinguishable from a locked door.

Return to our earlier definition: delusion is belief that has lost its refutability. In the Changwon case, the mother’s belief, “the outside world is dangerous”, could not be disproven because every piece of counter-evidence was pre-absorbed into the system. In the housing system, the state’s implicit belief, “debt reflects individual responsibility”, operates identically. When a family’s poverty is caused by policy (frozen benefits, inadequate social housing, the five-week Universal Credit wait), this is recoded that “They should have budgeted better.”

The debt rules do not ask why a family is in debt. They only ask whether the debt exists, and then structure is dissolved into individual pathology. This is thus institutional delusion. No amount of evidence about structural causation can enter the assessment framework and change the outcome.

The Disappearance of Hope

Return now to Cioran: “All gains are losses, and all losses are gains.” This is nihilism as saturation, and Cioran’s despair is also, paradoxically, a kind of honesty. He refuses the false comfort of premature closure, the delusion that we have found the answer, the system, the truth that ends all questioning.

The danger is what happens when institutions adopt the opposite stance while producing the same effect by claiming certainty while systematically eliminating the possibility of revision.

Hope, as I have defined it, is not optimism. It is the structural condition under which change remains possible(the willingness to be proven wrong). When hope dies in a family, we get Changwon. When hope dies in an institution, we get something harder to see but no less lethal: 159,000 children in “temporary” accommodation that has no exit.

Lauren Berlant called this cruel optimism: attachment to conditions that actually prevent flourishing. The families in temporary accommodation are told to be patient and to trust the process. However this is not hope but just a hope’s simulation, maintaining the appearance of openness while foreclosing.

Reopening the Room

If delusion is the collapse of refutability, then the task is not to find the “correct” belief but to rebuild the conditions under which beliefs can be changed. For science, it means authority must remain contestable. For epistemology more broadly, it means that hope must be institutionalised, as the structural commitment to keeping the world undetermined, to maintaining spaces where anomaly can accumulate and where judgement can be revised.

Conclusion

I began with a question: How do we determine who is deluded?

The answer is: we cannot determine this from any position of perfect externality. We are always inside some interpretive system. But this does not mean all systems are equivalent. The difference here is what the system does with doubt.

A delusion seals itself against doubt. On the other hand, living system metabolises doubt, allows it to produce crisis and change. The mother in Changwon tied the door handle with rope and the housing system ties the door with policy language. Both claim to be protecting something but both produce rooms without outside.

The task is therefore to ensure that doors, all doors, can still be opened, and with an outside.
