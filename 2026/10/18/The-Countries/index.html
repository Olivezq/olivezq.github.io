The Countries:A Memoir

A/N: What follows is a memoir, not an academic reflection.

I was born into a world whose ideas contradicted one another, and mine have turned over so many times since that I can no longer reach back to the child who held them.

In those years I still wrote 娘炮, sissy, about my friends in my diary. The cartoons I caught by chance, 喜羊羊与灰太狼 before it was revised, still handed down their small sermons, that a boy ought not to be petty, that a boy in tears cried like a girl. I can name now what runs wrong through all of it, though the child had no words for any of it, and the ideas ran through my days as plain fact, with nothing in them I ever thought to question.

Teachers told us the boys did better at science, and I believed them. When the punishments fell only on the boys, I grew more certain that boys were simply tougher, and we watched, cold and incurious, while desk and workbook collided with flesh, the boys by then a different order of creature, stilled and inert, nearer to a dead thing than to us.

A classmate said the Frog Princess disgusted her most, the darkest of them and so the ugliest. I was confused for the first time, since we had not yet come across anything like the idea of race. Primary school was an island where everyone was Asian, so we were only ever “people”, only ever “boys and girls”. I still remember what I thought, that people could like or dislike a face for its skin, and that the Frog Princess was to be pitied. Pressed to name the princess I hated back, I grabbed in a panic at Snow White and said it was for stealing the dwarves’ food, a reason that struck even me as thin, because that is really the story of Goldilocks and the three bears. The adults around me kept praising my skin for the palest in the room, though the praise bought me no pride, since I understood by then that it too came down to privilege, that what I was born into had never been mine to claim.

Through primary and middle school the boys and the girls both took me in, and I leaned toward the boys. In the 2010s a boy still stood above a girl, always appearing as the neutral default, while a girl was marked out as female. 女子如何不如男, the slogan went, a woman is in no way lesser than a man, and so the contempt never reached its worst, however a boy was still assumed to sit above a girl, and a girl was only encouraged to become “as good as a boy”, which was exactly how I was encouraged. I did not want to be a 假小子, a fake boy, and I did not want to be a 小女生 either, a word I had hated since I was small. Boys are always more interesting than girls, I used to think, and it took me years to turn the thought over and wonder whether the boys had only seemed more interesting because the world a girl was let into was so much smaller, and longer still to doubt the thought itself, and the tidy pair of words, man and woman, that it rested on.

My family counted as another of these privileges, since it narrowed the gap between my world and the boys’. Sexism and racism marked the house plainly enough, though by the measure of my primary school it ran almost as a model of openness, and my mother, who carried her own preference for boys, still pushed me out to play with the boys. She stood apart from the other mothers, further into idealism.

Coraline was the first film she ever set in front of me. She always told me it was my favourite, when the truth is she ran it over and over before I had grown enough to follow the pictures, a dozen times at the least, until its overcast idea of beauty moved into me ahead of any understanding, so that by the time I could follow the story the film had already settled in as the ground under my feet, and it comes nearer the truth to say Coraline laid claim to me than that I ever chose it. The scenes where the other mother turns I could not bring myself to watch for years. My mother loved, besides, a cartoon of talking fruit, one stretch of which lopped the head clean off a piece of fruit, and where it frightened me into shaking she only laughed and wound the tape back to run it again. Somewhere across those years the fogged bus window began to show me my own figure walking into a forest, the flat turning into a black castle that thorns stood guard over, the parrots out of my animal encyclopaedia lifting off the page to wheel through the rooms.

The cartoons reached me in English, without subtitles, and by that route she raised a bilingual world around me. Peppa Pig, Charlie and Lola, Gravity Falls. My school days ran in the city while my afternoons ran in an electronic country where I was as if in the West, so that when the girls traded talk of 叶罗丽 I had nothing to offer them. Years later, the first time I arrived in England, I felt at home at once, because I had in fact returned to the world of the cartoons. The world I had once been alone inside now lay in front of me, full of lively people, almost exactly as the cartoons had been. Only then did I see that Peppa Pig had grown out of the real world, that people really did take their children to pick berries on weekends, and really did drag an inflatable into the pool in summer to play in. I grew up inside two worlds split apart from one another.

A relative, once, turned my face up to the light and said, she is a girl after all, what a pity, her skin runs too dark, and then let a look of pity settle over her features.

Middle school brought me two others I fell in with, A and B. What drew the three of us together was nothing we could have named at the time, only that our sense of things lined up, and that in one another’s company a person could simply stay a person, with no boy or girl to answer to. To each other we were the ordinary ones, which is the quiet joke of it, since the world beyond our three kept its other names. It pinned 娘炮 and 人妖, sissy and freak, on A and B for the softness of their faces and shut them out of the boys, and it called me a boy outright, the girls telling me they wished I had been born one. Beside A and B I stopped having to place myself at all. Nothing in it handed me a position on gender, though it let me set down the need to hold one, and I used to wonder without ever once settling it, why the finding of them had fallen to me of all people, and whether the answer ran any deeper than a mother who had raised me, from the start, to keep an open mind.

In high school most of the girls were chasing idols, which left us with nothing to talk about, and I could not live inside a crowd that spent its days kissing and shrieking, while the boys ran to the open-contempt kind and I had no wish to talk to them. High school set my social skills back, since I spent most of my time reading novels and writing. The strangeness of the place came clear to me as my hallucinations shed their bright fantasy and let blood and insects and a slow horror seep in. I sank further into my own stories, where they had somewhere to go.

When I went to university, what I mostly felt was that I could relax. The difference between men and women had not disappeared, and people still lived in a world where being a woman was its own splendid thing and being a man was its own splendid thing, so the line between them remained, however the old constraints were gone, and every trait once sorted by sex was now admired instead, a woman is wonderful however she is, a man is wonderful however he is. It was also then that I first came across LGBTQ.

Only slowly did it come to me that the three of us in middle school had been, as it turned out, gay and bisexual and queer, though the labels arrived long after the friendship and explained none of it. Queer is more than I can lay over myself, and still I do not land as only male or only female. I count myself a woman, though that goes back to the sex I was born into, and some days I wonder whether what I am is a trans gay. Writing this, I find I cannot offer a clean conclusion, and that is about as far as my reading of myself goes to this day.
