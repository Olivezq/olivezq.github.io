On Violence and Its Frames

“Boy you don’t understand wude (小伙子你不讲武德).” — Ma Baoguo (马保国)

In May 2020, the self-styled grandmaster of “Hunyuan Xinyi Taiji”, a form of traditional Chinese kung fu he claimed could neutralise opponents twice his size, was knocked down three times in thirty seconds by an amateur martial artist in his late forties. The footage went viral and what followed was a reframe, stating that the young man had launched a surprise attack at the moment of Ma’s deliberate restraint, violating wude, the ethical code of the fighter. Ma had placed his fist near the young man’s nose without striking, “point to stop, as traditional kung fu demands (传统功夫以点到为止),” and been repaid with a punch to the eye. The loss, by this account, was not a loss at all but evidence of the opponent’s unworthiness.

The move is precise enough to be worth examining. By first establishing the norm that traditional kung fu operates through restraint, Ma changes defeat into moral vindication. Violence that works against one becomes proof of the other’s bad faith; violence one claim to have withheld becomes proof of mastery.

This is the structure of a particular kind of argument about violence: that it works, that the capacity for it is what ultimately determines outcomes, and that failures can always be attributed to rule-breaking rather than to any inadequacy in the premise. The argument is circular in a way that looks like evidence. Because the belief that violence is effective produces a disposition toward violent resolution, and violent resolution, when it works, confirms the belief, the loop closes without ever needing to test itself against a genuine counterexample. Benjamin‘s distinction between law-making and law-preserving violence captures something of this, that violence does not just disrupt order, it constitutes the order that in turn keeps producing the conditions under which violence looks necessary.

Whether this logic is specifically masculine is a question worth pausing on, not least because the answer is less obvious than it appears. The association between violence and masculine identity is real and well-documented, but “violence as the final arbiter of power” operates just as readily at the level of the state, the colonial administration, the development agency. Fanon‘s account of colonial violence, in which the colonised subject can only reclaim subjecthood through force, because every other avenue of self-assertion has been foreclosed, suggests that the relationship between violence and masculinity is situational. It is what happens when the capacity for violence becomes the only currency that the existing order will recognise. The question thus shifts from “is this masculine” to “what conditions make violence the only legible proof of power.”

Shrey Kapoor put pressure on this question in the Human Geography seminar this week, tracing the relationship between communal violence and urban development in Ahmedabad across the long twentieth century. His argument, compressed that anti-Muslim violence in Modi’s Gujarat was never only destructive. It was constitutive as it forged Hindu unity across caste lines, hardened the communal frontiers that became material walls, and produced the segregated spatial order that Hindu nationalist hegemony required as its operating environment. After the 2002 pogrom killed close to a thousand people, mostly Muslim, the visibility of state-backed violence became a liability. Investor anxiety and international visa bans showed that the violence had consolidated power but also revealed the fragility of the project. Modi’s response was to sublimate it. The Sabarmati Riverfront Project, relaunched under his direct control in 2003, displaced over 40,000 lower-caste Hindu and Muslim labourers who had lived on the riverbanks for generations. The evictions were framed as urban renewal. Thus the displaced became encroachers and their removal became progress.

Kapoor calls this communal capitalism, borrowing the logic of racial capitalism to argue that capital accumulation and communal violence are not parallel processes but the same process, where capitalist instruments serve communal ends and the violence of dispossession is rendered legible as development. The displaced populations, who had been the targets of riot violence for decades, became the targets of infrastructural dispossession, with the crucial difference that the removal now had a planning document behind it.

This is where the definition of violence starts to matter, and where a certain intellectual honesty is required. There is a legitimate objection to the way “violence” has expanded in recent decades such as “slow violence”, “symbolic violence”, “bureaucratic violence”, until it threatens to become a catch-all term that describes everything, in much the same way that “decolonisation” risks losing its analytical edge when applied metaphorically. If violence means a punch to the face and also means a planning document and also means a street renaming, the concept may be doing too much work, and the specificity of each form of harm gets flattened in the process.

Kapoor does not entirely escape this tension, but what makes his account more careful than the generic “development-as-violence” argument is that he traces the material connection between the two registers rather than simply asserting their equivalence. With the same populations and the same political project, riot violence and infrastructural dispossession were sequential operations, where the first created the conditions, eroding intercommunal trust, fracturing the local brokerage networks that had sustained mixed settlement, that made the second possible. Here the bureaucratic procedure was not a metaphor for violence. It was violence organised differently, requiring the prior violence to function.

Butler‘s analysis of frames of war becomes useful here, though again with some caution about what the framework is actually claiming. The point is not that all framings are equally arbitrary, but that the question of whose violence counts as violence, and whose counts as self-defence, or development, or the restoration of order, is never settled by the act itself. Ma Baoguo did not lose a fight and then describe it accurately. He lost a fight and then described it in a way that redistributed culpability. The woman in Kapoor’s account who sold her deed to a resettlement unit after being told that Muslims in the adjacent yard were throwing stones at Hindu residents (only to discover later that it was the community broker’s own entourage doing the throwing) was not deceived about a fact. She was deceived about the frame. The broker had manufactured the fear that made his services necessary, and the fear was real enough to act on regardless of its origin.

What this produces, taken together, is not quite the argument that violence always wins or that moral reasoning is merely decorative. It is in fact about the capacity to determine what counts as violence, and therefore what counts as legitimate response, is itself a form of power, and one that tends to accrue to whoever already controls the material conditions under which both the violence and its description occur. Ma Baoguo lost the fight. He has not, in any obvious sense, lost the argument about who didn’t observe wude. Whether that distinction matters depends on what one think the argument was ever about.
