Constructing Humanity:On The Politics of World Heritage

On the fourth of March, the LSE Department of International Relations hosted a book launch for Elif Kalaycioglu’s The Politics of World Heritage: Visions, Custodians, and Futures of Humanity, published by Oxford University Press. Kalaycioglu is a lecturer at King’s College London, and the book draws on decades of archival research and fieldwork within the UNESCO World Heritage regime, the institutional machinery that decides, since 1972, which sites belong to humanity.

The opening problem the book poses is deceptively simple. The World Heritage Convention asserts that certain places in the world are of such outstanding interest that they must be preserved as part of the heritage of mankind as a whole. But mankind, as a cultural and historical subject with a shared memory, does not exist prior to that assertion. It has to be made. Kalaycioglu’s argument is that the regime has been making it and remaking it for over fifty years, and that the social and political orders which emerge from those constructions are neither neutral nor stable.

The analysis moves through three periods, each organised around a distinct vision of humanity. In the regime’s early decades, humanity was conceived as a rarefied, transhistorical subject: the depositary of the highest forms of human creativity and aesthetic genius. The criteria for outstanding universal value, formulated by ICOMOS in 1976 and shaped almost entirely by Western European and North American experts, valorised monumentality, grand civilisational artefacts, urban planning, and the progressive arc of world history. The heritage list that emerged from these criteria was, predictably, a map of a humanity whose long stay and centre of gravity was in Europe. Colonial cities in South America were praised as demonstrations of ingenuity in the adoption of universal principles to local conditions, a framing that made post-colonial states’ claims to heritage legible only through the terms of the very history that had dispossessed them. A handwritten note from UNESCO’s then Director-General Amadou-Mahtar M’Bow, attached to an internally circulated memo, urged that the evaluation criteria not be established on the basis of cultural designs valid only for part of the world. That call went unheeded.

The second period brought a paradigm shift, driven by a subgroup of experts, anthropologists, and Australian and New Zealand specialists who had been working through domestic contestations around indigenous heritage, who introduced new resources into the regime’s symbolic universe about vernacular architecture, know-how, living cultures, harmonious human-nature relations. The vision of humanity shifted from the rarefied to the diverse. Sites previously rejected or invisible became legible. Zanzibar, once dismissed for preserving only recent vestiges of its history, was reinscribed as demonstrating an outstanding fusion of cultures. Indigenous sites in Australia and New Zealand, previously recognised only as natural heritage, acquired cultural value. The shift was, on the whole, a positive development, but it arrived with its own hierarchies. An early workshop organised for African experts illustrates the tension. One European expert praised the turn toward anthropological parameters as finally allowing African heritage to be represented on its own terms; another described that same heritage, in the same meeting, as possessing the haunting charm of human being at its prime, the language of appreciation that carries the logic of primitivism.

The third period, from around 2010, brought a different kind of assertion. States began positioning themselves as the proper adjudicators, contributors, and custodians of world heritage, and through it humanity. Expert evaluations were increasingly overturned at committee meetings. The vision of a diverse humanity was retained, but firmly attached to the nation-state as its natural representative, diversity as the sum of sovereign parts. China’s revitalisation of the Silk Roads heritage, tightly tethered to the Belt and Road Initiative within the regime’s symbolic universe, is the clearest example. It is a world-historical narrative of peaceful trade and exchange, through which China positions itself as a subject that has steered a diverse humanity’s global relations before and can do so again. The irony Kalaycioglu identifies is structural. In their pursuit of the prestige that world heritage confers, a prestige grounded precisely in the more-than-national character of outstanding universal value, states risk collapsing the very distinction that generates the prestige. If humanity is the aggregate of nation-states, adjudicated by states as such, heritage can never be more than an internationally endorsed national honour roll.

What keeps the regime from fully dissolving into that contradiction, Kalaycioglu suggests, is that states continue to employ the symbolic resources of the convention even as they assert their sovereign prerogatives. They know, as she puts it, that they are breaking the toy and that they should not break it entirely if they want to keep playing with it. Peace and solidarity remain in circulation as legitimising frames, even under conditions of increasing state centrism. The nomination file for the Bikini Atoll Nuclear Test Site, submitted by the Republic of Marshall Islands, offers what Kalaycioglu calls a nested and uneven construction of humanity, one that holds together the global significance of nuclear weapons technology with the radically unequal distribution of their consequences across populations. It does not dispense with the universality, but it does not flatten the hierarchy either. Whether this kind of construction can do more political work than the regime’s dominant modes remains, at the end of the book, an open question.

The discussion that followed the presentation circled back repeatedly to the problem of adjudication, about how outstanding universal value can be determined at all once the symbolic universe has been expanded to include vernacular and living cultures, which resist the traceable monumentality that made the original criteria legible. Kalaycioglu’s answer was: the expert reports have become more scientifically elaborate precisely to speak to an uncertainty they cannot resolve. The aspiration and the method are not, at this point, hanging neatly together. The regime continues to function, and the list continues to grow, but what it is doing and for whom, remains a question the institution is not well-positioned to answer from inside itself.
