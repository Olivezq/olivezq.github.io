March 8, 2026

For All Women

Today is the eighth of March. It is also the ninth day of the war between the United States and Iran, in which at least 1,332 Iranian civilians have died so far, the majority of them in strikes on schools and hospitals.

The United Nations theme for this year’s International Women’s Day is “Rights, Justice, Action — for all women and girls.” The word all is doing a great deal of work here.

Earlier this week, I attended a webinar hosted by the Peace Economy Project, a fellowship programme I am part of as a decolonising research fellow, on the transfer of militarised frameworks from foreign policy into domestic governance. The panellists traced a consistent mechanism, that the label precedes the logic. Call someone a narco-terrorist, and the response is no longer law enforcement but warfare. Call protesters enemies from within, and the National Guard becomes a legitimate instrument of domestic order. The language, as one panellist put it, “accustoms people to think about martial responses to what are social problems.” What the label does, structurally, is remove the labelled person from the category of those who warrant protection, from due process and from the presumption of civilian status, and place them in the category of those against whom protection is exercised.

This is the mechanism the news cycle has been circling around today from several directions at once. In the United Kingdom, the Supreme Court ruled last year that transgender women do not fall within the legal definition of “woman” under the Equality Act, a decision whose consequences for trans people’s daily lives PinkNews has been documenting throughout. In the United States, three cases before the Supreme Court this year will determine whether conversion therapy bans violate free speech, whether trans girls can be excluded from women’s sport, and whether such exclusions breach the Fourteenth Amendment’s equal protection clause. In France, a coalition of feminist unions called today for a national strike, from work and from domestic labour, on the grounds that women still perform the majority of unpaid care while holding a fraction of legal and economic power. The UN puts the figure at 64% of men’s legal rights globally.

These share a structural logic, that protection of a defined category requires the prior act of defining who falls within it, and that act of definition is never politically neutral. The border of all women is not a natural perimeter and it is in fact drawn and contested.

What the PEP webinar made clear is that this drawing of borders accelerates under militarised conditions. When emergency powers are declared or the insurrection Act is invoked, the institutions that normally adjudicate who belongs where are progressively sidelined. One of the panellists noted that since 1979 there has been an uninterrupted national emergency regarding Iran still technically on the books; that emergency powers, once activated, almost never fully expire; and that the courts have historically been deferential to the executive on anything framed in terms of national security. The emergency state does not suspend the question of who counts. It just answers it faster, with less deliberation, and in favour of whoever currently holds the power to answer.

Each case here shares a similar position, that each is subject to a system that defines the scope of its own protection, and each falls, in different ways and to different degrees, outside the line that system has drawn. What the International Women‘s Day cannot do, from within its own framework, is resolve the tension between all as aspiration and all as the thing that keeps being renegotiated. That tension is the actual political condition the occasion is marking.
