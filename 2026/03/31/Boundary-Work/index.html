Boundary Work and the Dialectics of Platform Identity

A Reflection on Cammaerts’ Dichotomies in Media and Communication Theory

At the launch of his new textbook on 31 March 2026, Bart Cammaerts opened with a scene from this same LSE theatre. Keynes and Hayek in 1931, locked in a debate whose very structure, binary, adversarial, performed as intellectual duel, became his object of inquiry. The book maps eight dichotomies that organise media and communication studies, and Cammaerts’ central provocation is methodological. He refuses to resolve these tensions, arguing instead for inhabiting “the lure of the middle,” a position he describes as dialectical rather than conciliatory. Two contradictory things, he insists, can be true at the same time.

This claim has a longer genealogy than Cammaerts’ own framing through Gramsci, Mouffe, and Foucault might suggest. The symmetry principle in Bloor’s strong programme makes a structurally analogous move, demanding that we explain “true” and “false” knowledge claims using the same causal apparatus, suspending the epistemological privilege that would let us dismiss one side of a dichotomy in advance. Where Cammaerts applies this logic to political economy versus cultural studies, or Habermasian rationalism versus agonistic democracy, GY220 applies it to technological determinism versus social constructivism.

The terrain matters, because Cammaerts’ most compelling empirical material turns out to be legible in STS terms in ways that exceed his own theoretical vocabulary. Platforms, he argues, are simultaneously media and communication infrastructure. They make editorial decisions through algorithmic curation while claiming the legal status of neutral conduits modelled on the telephone network. This double identity allows them to evade the regulatory obligations imposed on legacy media. Cammaerts reads this as ideological manoeuvre. Read through Gieryn’s concept of boundary work, the analysis sharpens. Platform companies are drawing and redrawing the boundary between “media” and “communication tool” depending on the regulatory context, performing demarcation whose direction is consistently self-serving. The dichotomy is not a pre-existing analytical category that platforms happen to fall between. It is being produced and maintained through specific legal and commercial practices.

Jasanoff’s idiom of co-production pushes this further. The platform’s technical architecture and its juridical identity are co-produced. The legal framework enables a particular technological design, and the technological design reinforces the plausibility of the legal claim. The effect is a systemic misrecognition in which algorithmic editorial power becomes invisible because the institutional apparatus classifies it as something else. The parallel to environmental governance is direct. When carbon footprint calculators individualise a structural problem, or when cost-benefit analysis translates incommensurable ecological loss into a commensurate metric, the same mechanism operates. A technical instrument produces a particular way of seeing that forecloses others, and the foreclosure is experienced as objectivity.

Cammaerts’ discussion of the GDPR crystallises this. He observes that Europe’s most robust privacy regulation amounts, in practice, to users consenting to their own surveillance. Refusal means exclusion from the service. The architecture of the consent interface enacts what Winner would recognise as politics embedded in technical design, where a structural power asymmetry is presented as individual choice. GY220 names this responsibilisation, the displacement of systemic accountability onto individual conduct. It is the same logic that underpins personal carbon footprint discourse and “media literacy” as the primary policy answer to platform harms. What STS adds is a vocabulary for understanding how it is technically accomplished, through material configurations that make one framing appear natural while rendering alternatives unthinkable.

Where the lecture left productive gaps was where Cammaerts’ normative commitments outpaced his analytical framework. His call for a “public service internet” is compelling as political aspiration. As a sociotechnical proposal, it remains underspecified. Jasanoff would ask what epistemic infrastructure such a project requires, whose expertise would be authoritative in its design, and how the forms of public participation governing its development might themselves reproduce existing power asymmetries. These are constitutive of whether the imaginary transforms or reproduces the dynamics Cammaerts critiques.

The deepest resonance between his project and GY220 may be methodological. Both arrive, from different disciplinary starting points, at the same conclusion. Binary oppositions are analytically generative when we refuse to resolve them. Cammaerts reaches this through critical political theory. STS reaches it through the empirical study of how knowledge and social order are mutually constituted. That convergence suggests that the instability of dichotomies is not a problem to be solved but a condition that reveals how power operates through the act of classification itself.
