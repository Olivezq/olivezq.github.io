The Invisible Heart:On the Care Economy and Social Housing

On the third of March, LSE Housing and Communities hosted a public panel on the relationship between the care economy and social housing, featuring Anne Power, emeritus professor of social policy at LSE and author of Beyond Bricks and Mortar; Tim Jackson, ecological economist at the University of Surrey and author of The Care Economy; and Jonathon Porritt, writer and long-time campaigner on sustainable development. The three panellists had sat together on the UK Sustainable Development Commission for nearly a decade, which is a shared history that gave the conversation an unusual density of reference, and an occasional candour about the limits of what they had managed to achieve.

Anne Power opened with a deceptively simple claim that social housing is not just an instance of the care economy but one of its clearest illustrations. The argument moves through history. From medieval almshouses, of which around three thousand survive in England today, and whose residents live on average two years longer than the general population, not despite their age and poverty but, Power argues, because of the quality of care organised around them, through Robert Owen’s New Lanark, Octavia Hill’s tenant management experiments, the Quaker industrial villages, and the Peabody Trust, a consistent logic appears. Housing that works is housing that is maintained and embedded in a community structure that takes ongoing responsibility for both the physical fabric and the people within it. The nineteenth-century models, for all their paternalism, understood this. The twentieth-century state, in Power’s telling, largely forgot it. Between the wars and through to 1980, sixty-five million council homes were built, a third of total stock, by administrations too preoccupied with construction targets to attend to management. As the most disadvantaged households were concentrated into the newest and least desirable estates, council housing became what planners called difficult to let. The housing associations that had operated alongside councils, small and community-rooted, were then drawn into the same logic, that pressured to absorb large-scale stock transfers, forced to borrow from private markets, they became developers, and the caring economy came into conflict with itself.

Tim Jackson’s contribution took the argument to a different register. His guiding question, “what can prosperity possibly mean on a finite planet,” reframes the problem entirely. The conventional answer, that prosperity is wealth and wealth is accumulation, breaks down not only under ecological constraints but under its own internal contradictions. When Jackson asked people directly what prosperity meant to them, health came out at or near the top. Health of self, family, community, environment. What would follow, he asked, if we took that seriously, if we organised the economy around health rather than wealth? The analogy he reaches for is physiological, a slow breath rather than hyperventilation. Care, in this frame is, as the sociologist Joan Tronto put it, everything we do to maintain or repair our world so that we can live in it as well as possible. It lies at what Nancy Folbre calls the invisible heart of the economy, that without it, there is no economy at the most basic level, yet it is systematically undervalued and feminised. The employment-intensive sectors closest to care have the lowest material and environmental footprints. They represent, Jackson argues, a kind of economic sweet spot, a blueprint for a wellbeing economy. Care’s virtues are capitalism’s vices. Employment intensity reads, in mainstream economics, as low productivity growth. Wages follow productivity, so care workers are condemned to precarious conditions and low status, while the extractive logic of financialisation strips value from the sectors care depends on.

Porritt brought the ideological genealogy. The care deficit, he argued, did not accumulate by accident or through a sequence of bad decisions. Its roots lie in the Mont Pelerin Society, founded in 1947, and the slow maturation of neoliberal ideas through the 1950s and 1960s until the moment, around 1979, when they became policy. Three mechanisms in particular: the progressive marketisation of the state at every level; the licensed externalisation of costs from private balance sheets onto society; and the financialisation of the economy, whose consequences in the care sector are now visible in the figures for children in care, cost per child rising from £160,000 to £320,000 per annum over twenty years, driven not by better outcomes but by debt servicing, executive bonuses, and the architecture of private finance. Porritt was more direct than the others about what this represents.

The discussion that followed circled around two questions the panellists could not quite resolve. The first was historical: when, if ever, had a British government genuinely placed care at the centre of its politics? The answers offered, Clement Attlee, perhaps the early years of New Labour, perhaps neither, suggested more about the limits of the political imagination than about any recoverable tradition. The second was structural: whether a single country could transition toward a care-oriented economy within a global system still organised around extractive growth. Jackson’s position was cautious but not entirely pessimistic: care is typically local and non-traded, which means it can be nurtured locally even under adverse international conditions. Porritt was less sanguine, the analogy to climate action held, he thought, and the conclusion was similar: you cannot decouple unilaterally from a system whose logic pervades every level of governance and finance.

What remained unresolved at the end of the evening was the question of mechanism. The diagnosis that the logic of accumulation destroys the logic of care, that housing becomes dysfunctional when it becomes an asset class, that care workers are structurally condemned to bad conditions by a productivity metric rigged against them, was stated clearly and from multiple directions. What a transition toward something different would actually require, whether in housing policy, in care sector regulation, or in the deeper architecture of how the economy values work, was gestured at rather than answered.
