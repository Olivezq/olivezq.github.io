Recent Readings on Environmental Studies

by “recent” I mean works published after 2025

This blog collects a number of recently published works on environmental issues, spanning environmental economics, policy analysis, the environmental humanities, and philosophy. Each of these contributions has its own distinctive perspective, engaging with questions of psychology, gender, migration, sustainability, and beyond.

It should be noted that these recommendations do not imply any particular political stance or value judgment on my part. They are shared purely out of personal reading interest and academic exploration. Much like tasting different desserts, recommending a strawberry cake does not mean I dislike blueberry cake, what follows is simply part of an ongoing process of exploration.

Another note: the depth of commentary here is not evenly balanced across disciplines. The sections on environmental economics are relatively concise, since such studies often rely on model derivations and policy comparisons, with conclusions leaning toward objective outcomes that leave limited space for personal commentary. By contrast, works in the environmental humanities and philosophy deal with emotions, identity, culture, and symbolism, dimensions that more naturally invite subjective reflection and multi-layered interpretation. As a result, those sections are presented in greater detail. This unevenness does not signal any dismissal of the importance of economic research.

I. Environmental Economics and Policy

Chen, T., & Karney, D. H. (2025, January 6). Environmental policy in general equilibrium under market power and price discrimination. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2501.03114

This paper, published in January 2025, pairs well with Burani & Mantovani (2025) (and GY222). Both address price discrimination in the context of environmental economics, but they approach the issue from different angles.

Chen & Karney develop a new model that incorporates oligopoly markets and third-degree price discrimination, comparing the welfare effects of instruments such as emission taxes, two-part tariffs, and other environmental policies. They show that distortions created by price discrimination can complicate the trade-off with policies designed to correct externalities. The paper is heavy on mathematical derivations, very much in line with what one might encounter in an economics exam setting.

Burani, N., & Mantovani, A. (2025). Environmental policies with green network effect and price discrimination. Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 235, 107043. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2025.107043

Published in July 2025, this article introduces an innovative framework that models green products as having both hedonic and environmental attributes, while also incorporating green network effects. The authors conclude that achieving social optimality requires a combination of emission taxes and income-differentiated subsidies.

The argumentation is clear, and the paper contains a self-aware discussion of its limitations. It stands as a representative example of a very well-crafted academic study.

II. Environmental Humanities and Identity Politics

Perret, M. (2025). Migration Is Natural: Monarch Butterflies as Symbols of Environmental Justice and Queer Migrant Identity in Art-Activism. Environmental Humanities, 17(2), 371–401. https://read.dukeupress.edu/environmental-humanities/article/17/2/371/401638

Perret examines monarch butterflies as a symbolic nexus through which to explore the entanglements of species migration, human migration, identity politics, and environmental justice. The article engages with Latinx feminism, Latinx environmentalism, queer migration, and related strands of thought.

Reading this, I was reminded of Wald et al. (2019) Latinx Environmentalisms, Wright (2019), and Chávez (2013) Queer Migration Politics, all of which provide rich intertextual context for questions of identity, borders, and ecological justice.

Although butterflies are sometimes criticized as an overused motif, they remain a powerful cultural symbol. Across different contexts, their symbolism becomes even more multifaceted:

  • In Latin American contexts, mariposa is a slur for gay men (Perret, 2025, p. 381).

  • In Chinese contexts, a science video once compared monarchs to an “oppressed husband” and milkweed to a “toxic wife,” a stark contrast to Western feminist readings.

  • In popular culture and conspiracy lore, monarchs have also become entangled with “MK-Ultra/Monarch Programming” imagery, often used as metaphors for dissociation and control in fashion, music videos, and celebrity iconography.

These overlapping narratives show that butterflies are not inherently meaningful; rather, their symbolism is continually re-inscribed by cultural contexts.

For me personally, the symbolism resonates beyond text and culture. In middle school, I raised a brood of Vanessa Indica as part of a biology club project. That experience gave me a direct appreciation of their ecological behavior: for instance, many butterflies feed on carrion, and large gatherings often indicate the death of a nearby animal. Such ecological facts could themselves be mobilized as metaphors: “corruption and renewal,” or “death and congregation.”

In this sense, Perret’s work also shows how natural species become sites of intersecting cultural imaginations. Their meanings are never fixed but continually generated in new contexts. This is part of what makes the environmental humanities compelling: through the “migration” of species, we are invited to reconsider the boundaries of human societies and identities.

Sköld, A. (2025). Climate Melancholia. Environmental Humanities, 17(2), 449–468. https://read.dukeupress.edu/environmental-humanities/article/17/2/449/401629

Sköld introduces the concept of climate melancholia, drawing on psychoanalytical metapsychology and Judith Butler’s reflections on grief and collective action, to examine how the climate crisis reshapes human affect and social responses. In Freudian psychoanalysis, melancholia carries a specific technical meaning (distinguished from mourning), but as I do not have a psychology background, I will refrain from elaborating to avoid misrepresentation.

Climate change produces varied psychological effects—anxiety, depression, grief—differentiated across geography, socioeconomic status, and other intersecting factors. Sköld emphasizes that our present crisis stems not from an excess of grief but from a refusal or inability to grieve: “the central problem at the heart of climate melancholia is precisely that most people do not weep. We do not weep because we do not grieve, and we do not grieve because we do not really care.” (p. 10). The phrasing, with its recursive structure, delivers a striking clarity and weight.

What stands out most is Sköld’s rhetorical strategy. He repeatedly uses “we,” drawing readers into a shared community rather than maintaining analytical distance. This invites identification and emotional involvement. For instance: “Even though there are perceptual, cognitive, and emotional barriers blocking a direct experience of ecological losses, our ability to grieve and ways of grieving are not hardwired into our DNA.” (p. 4). On the surface, this is a cognitive-scientific claim, yet the phrasing evokes a sense of loss, as if grief itself were being enacted in the reader’s experience. Sköld does not merely describe grief, but his language compels readers to feel it. This transformation of an abstract concept into an embodied affect is remarkable.

The article concludes: “Genuine hope rests upon a confrontation with a wounded reality in a mournful way while keeping the horizon for future possibilities and change open.” (p. 14). Throughout, the prose builds both rigor and emotional cadence, guiding readers to recognize mourning not as despair’s terminus but as a condition for solidarity and transformative action.

What I find most precious here is that the logical precision is intertwined with emotional resonance. Reading it becomes not only an intellectual but also a psychological experience, that the weight of melancholia was accompanied by a gradual ignition of hope.

Update (October 2, 2025): I realize that this piece resonates intriguingly with Afroogh, Mostafavi, and Jiao’s (2024) proposal of Intelligent Environmental Empathy (IEE). While Sköld approaches climate melancholia through psychoanalysis and the difficulty of collective grieving, Afroogh et al. highlight the structural failure of top-down international cooperation in addressing climate justice. One articulates the affective conditions necessary for solidarity, the other sketches a technological and institutional mechanism through which that solidarity might be operationalized.

III. Philosophy and Cross-Scalar Thinking

Likavčan, L. (2025). The Grass of the Universe: Rethinking Technosphere, Planetary History, and Sustainability with Fermi Paradox. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2411.08057

Philosopher Lukáš Likavčan employs the Fermi paradox and SETI as conceptual tools to reconsider the relationship between the technosphere and the biosphere/planetary history. He suggests that habitability and genesity may be more fruitful concepts than the conventional sustainability in describing conditions for life on Earth. For those interested, it is worth exploring in detail why genesity might capture dimensions that sustainability cannot.

This piece enriches posthuman ecological discourse and invites cross-scalar thinking. It also resonates intriguingly with Perret’s work: while Perret examines migration across regions and identities, Likavčan gestures toward the possibility of migration across planets.

Conclusion

Taken together, these works reveal that environmental issues are never confined to technical or policy dimensions alone; they are deeply embedded in human culture, identity, and affective life. Whether through economic modeling, philosophical reframing, or artistic and symbolic analysis, each offers a distinct lens for grappling with contemporary ecological challenges.
