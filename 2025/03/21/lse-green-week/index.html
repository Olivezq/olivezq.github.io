Experience of LSE Green Week:no experience

Maybe I went at times when few people visited, or maybe it was just like that. I went three times, and each time, the exhibition in MAR felt deserted. But in fact, I really liked the concept: using artistic expression to alleviate stress, blending rational reflection with emotional creativity.

You could see it right as you entered MAR (though by now it’s probably gone). There were boards arranged in a circle, representing different departments—Social Policy, Gender Studies, Law, International Development, and others. Each displayed ideas about how their fields could contribute to sustainable development. Compared to many universities, LSE does seem to take sustainability quite seriously.

At the center, there was a whiteboard covered with Post-it notes and magazine cutouts, presumably filled with students’ thoughts on sustainability. Unfortunately, it seemed the exhibition simply aimed to collect these ideas without responding to them. Many of the comments, from what I read, reflected misunderstandings—or even perpetuated misconceptions—about sustainability, or at least deserved deeper engagement. It made me wish someone had been there to guide the conversation. “Helping people understand the diverse meanings of sustainability” should be the true point of Green Week, rather than simply encouraging a uniform chant of “we must protect the environment.”

If you’re going to talk about sustainable development, you can’t just talk about sustainable development. You also have to talk about environment, economy, social justice. You have to talk about how much of urban development invokes slogans like “living in harmony with nature” merely as marketing, to raise economic value; how declarations of “development” sometimes exist primarily to let the First World feel righteous. You have to talk about how everyone’s idea of sustainability differs, how every policy defines it differently, how the “better world” each person imagines is never the same. Sustainable development can’t accommodate everyone equally—just as “you can never truly eliminate pollution,” you also can’t eliminate inequality, or poverty.

Some people believe that buying eco-friendly products counts as sustainable development. That is, in a sense, their definition. But behind the intention to pursue sustainability, behind the motivations to commercialize sustainability, the path of life is far more complex.

Setting aside the missed opportunities, I was touched by two yellow Post-it notes on the whiteboard quoting Michael Jackson’s lyrics: “We are the world, we are the children.” It beautifully echoed a passage by Chinese writer Shi Tiesheng:

“Countless souls drifting in the universe, finally seeing a light in the vastness; ‘We are the world, we are the children’—and thus they are drawn, they converge there, toward the radiance of that voice. That is belonging, call it what you will, Buddhism or God.”

Whether it’s Buddhism, or God, or Nature, or Sustainable Development itself—faith hovers above daily life. And thus the familiar blessing remains: “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed.”
