Academic Reflection
May 30, 2025

Between Coloniality and Tree Bark Stains

Reflections on The Geopolitics of Green Colonialism[1]

I stumbled upon this new book in a bookstore days ago. I thought I’d look for a PDF version later, but found nothing online (even though there’s an ISBN). So after finishing my final exam, I went back to flip through it. In the first chapter, the authors mention their intention to include as many voices from the Global South and Global North as possible. Indeed, the list of contributors is vast like an ocean. But I quickly noticed one glaring absence: China.

Was it a deliberate exclusion? I don’t think so. More likely, there simply isn’t anyone in China contributing to this discussion. Or perhaps it’s because the academic landscape in China tends to handle the concept of “coloniality” with a high degree of abstraction and political sensitivity. This reminded me of a similar thought I had while reading Artery on May 14[2]:

I don’t want to confine myself to Western coloniality alone. I want to bring in the question of what coloniality might look like in the Chinese context.

Yet, as I try to delve deeper into this topic, I find myself stuck. Research on coloniality in China seems to have no clear entry point. We’re constantly being “fed” narratives of colonialism and anti-colonialism from the West, whether from the colonizer’s side or the colonized. I’m increasingly aware of how the knowledge I consume may already be shaped by a specific logic.

So I want to actively seek the voices of outsiders, those not molded by central narratives. Only then do I have a chance to move beyond Western-critical theory templates and form observations rooted in my own context.

One of the book’s endorsers is Kothari, author of one of the essential readings in GY121[3]. Meanwhile, the same day, in CKK at LSE, I came across the names Atkinson and Dietz, another group of authors from GY121[4]. In that moment, I grasped the “network behind the names”: many of these conversations aren’t just conceptual; they are spatially situated. Their offices are literally close together.

This coincidence, yet inevitability, made me again realize that academia isn’t some abstract tower. It’s geographic, relational, and embedded in human networks. It’s a kind of intellectual geopolitics.

That morning, I also read Tigers and the City That Never Sleeps by Chen Zhiwei[5]. His language is intensely sensitive and synesthetic. I kept wondering: can I still write like that? Has my vocabulary thinned? Have I lost my synesthesia?

I looked up and saw yellow blood flowing from the fork of a plane tree, it moved dynamically, but it was actually only bark stains. The sensations were still within me and daily life had dulled them. But Chen’s perspective infected mine. I began to imagine more things like something I recorded in late April last year: A bird was missing one wing. Its black feathers left a white streak near the tail, like fingerless gloves. Its eyes were pitch black, glinting in the yellow light. Sadly, it was just an ordinary, injured magpie, helpless under the spring sun. On the ground, I found its other wing. At first, I thought it was a piece of plastic, but the wind didn’t move it. I crouched down and saw the same pattern as the intact wing. A severed wing. It fluttered weakly. People bustled by, stepping on its remains.

Walking back to Passfield Hall, I heard a woman screaming from a UCL building across the street. The scream was piercing and lasted a long time. Nobody stopped.

Later at home, I stumbled upon a video of a little girl and her grandma making noodles. She rode her little tricycle, called out to the moon granny, lived in a small countryside house. She looked so innocent, with the kind of ideal childhood that feels almost mythical now. She was lucky. Incredibly lucky. That’s what a family should look like.

In that moment, I realized: perhaps this is what it means to be, not oppressed by systems, not entangled in colonial critiques, just living. The world is already so full of oppression toward gender, nature, race. And yet, some people are still able to grow up this loved, this carefree.

Even in this world, where internet control suppresses voices, where status varies, where environmentalism is often capitalist greenwashing, a little girl can still be loved, still live in a world full of play and innocence. People are so different. Lives are so vastly different.

People always love to complain, about how hard life is, how broken the world is, how awful others are, as if all the problems exist outside themselves. Yet, as the Festinger principle goes, we can’t control 10% of what happens in life, but we can control how we respond to that 10%, which affects the other 90%. Human consciousness is bigger than we imagine. Often, all we need is continuous reflection, fantasy, vision and we can resurrect ourselves.

We only need to walk toward our light because everyone deserves that purity.

I say this knowing how much easier it is to say than to do. I often feel like the priest in a confession booth. But still, I must keep asking myself: from books, scholars, names, videos, tree stains, screams, Chen Zhiwei, Atkinson, Kothari, all of it leads me to one question:

What about me?

Appendix Notes

Since I’ve added Anthropocrevice as a tag, I thought I’d take this opportunity to briefly explain the term.

Anthropocrevice｜人类隙

A hybrid word derived from Anthropocene and crevice. Usable as both noun and adjective.

It refers to a specific emotional state: a psychological fissure in which sorrow, detachment, and macro-scale awareness coexist. It occurs when an individual, as a conscious being situated in the “Anthropocene”, this fleeting crevice in planetary history, suddenly perceives their own smallness and yet simultaneous entanglement within vast systems.

You might experience an anthropocrevice when reading, or simply walking through the city, seeing the sky, trees, or a squirrel. These are no longer mere images, but fragments of the ecological, civilizational, and historical structure you inhabit.

It can be a subtle empathy toward others’ suffering, or a melancholic witness to the rhythm of the world. It is a moment of realization:

“I see myself as interconnected with them, with nature, with existence, and I, too, am but a fleeting crevice in the age of humankind.”
