Rivers and Power:Critical Reflections

Rivers and Power: Critical Reflections on Artery and Colonial Ecologies

Author’s Note: This post is informed by my engagement with today’s discussion at LSE and the close reading of Austin Zeiderman’s book Artery. It does not seek to function as a conventional review, but rather as a reflective intervention into ongoing conversations surrounding voice, methodology, and scholarly responsibility within the discourse of colonial ecologies. These reflections emerge from my own situated perspective and inevitably reflect my personal interpretation of the material—subject to limitation, incompleteness, or misunderstanding. I welcome correction and further dialogue, and offer these thoughts with humility and respect for the complexity of the issues and the work under consideration.

In his discussion, Dr.Zeiderman situated Colombia’s Magdalena River as a site through which to interrogate the mutually constitutive relationships among race, capital, and ecological transformation. His central contention was that river governance and state-sponsored infrastructural projects are not ideologically neutral undertakings but rather contemporary manifestations of enduring colonial spatial and racial regimes. The river, in this account, emerges not as a passive natural object, but as an active site of sociopolitical contestation and ecological inscription.

In Artery, Zeiderman explicitly acknowledges his positionality as an external researcher and reflects on his own racialized and epistemic location. He links his critical engagement with inequality to a personal genealogy shaped by racial mobility in the United States. This level of reflexivity is both thoughtful and admirable.

Yet, such reflections inevitably prompt further inquiry:

When a Global North-based scholar interprets colonial histories through the analytic of “racial ecologies,” do we encounter the subaltern speaking, or are we witnessing representational mediation through epistemic frames external to those communities?

Can Decolonial Critique Be Articulated from Within the Metropole?

Among the questions raised during the event was a particularly resonant one: Can infrastructure ever be decolonial? This question interrogates not only the book’s analytic emphasis on racialized logistics but also broader debates within critical theory regarding the epistemological and ethical constraints of structural critique.

Dr.Zeiderman’s response foregrounded the analytic nature of his inquiry, as distinct from normative or programmatic prescriptions. Nonetheless, such a position invites critical scrutiny:

When scholarship confines itself to exposition without praxis-oriented engagement, does it risk replicating the epistemic hierarchies it seeks to unveil?

Epistemic Extractivism and the Politics of Translation

The monograph offers a compelling ethnographic account of fluvial laborers whose intimate, embodied knowledge of the river is both intergenerational and deeply contextual. These descriptions are sensitively rendered and provide valuable insight into the lived experience of riverine ecologies.

Yet, the epistemological architecture of the book relies predominantly on Euro-American theoretical frameworks, raising important questions regarding the translation and codification of local knowledges. This suggests a persistent asymmetry between empirical subject and conceptual authority—a dynamic that warrants sustained reflection.

To be clear, this is not a critique of individual scholarly intent but of the structural conditions that shape knowledge production in contemporary academia.

Regionalism, Structuralism, and the Limits of Positional Reflexivity

Artery productively explores how Colombian geographic imaginaries have historically reframed colonial racial taxonomies into regional hierarchies. Zeiderman’s attempt to intersect regionalism with ecological materialism yields an intellectually generative reading of spatialized race.

However, certain analytical tendencies within the text remain primarily structural in orientation, with limited engagement with subaltern epistemologies, including Indigenous cosmologies and land-based relationalities. Attending to these perspectives may prove vital for advancing a truly decolonial ecological critique.

Towards a Pluriversal Ecology of Knowledge

Liboiron has insisted that “pollution is not a metaphor but a material mechanism of colonialism.” Following this logic, we must also acknowledge that scholarly discourse is not ontologically neutral.

In an academic landscape increasingly attentive to knowledge flows from the Global South, we might do well to ask with greater urgency:

Who speaks? By what epistemic means? And for whose benefit?

These questions are not posed as accusations but as an invitation to collectively interrogate the ethics of scholarly voice, representation, and authority.

Conclusion: Dr.Zeiderman’s longstanding engagement with Latin American contexts, and the ethical care embedded in Artery, are evident and praiseworthy. The questions raised here are not intended as dismissals but rather as prompts for further dialogue—with the hope of advancing critical discourse at the intersection of ecology, coloniality, and knowledge.

May “artery” signify not only the channels of imperial capital and infrastructural governance but also the lifelines through which decolonial epistemologies and alternative futures might circulate.
