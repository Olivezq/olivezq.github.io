November 13, 2025

Edges of Care, Zones of the Dead

The map is more ruthless than people imagine. Physical proximity is not the same as political visibility; institutional distance is often far more lethal than spatial distance. Who is included, who is omitted, who is pushed beyond the boundary, these are forms of violence geography exerts at seemingly ordinary scales.

Care is, at its core, allocation, as the system decides who is seen and who is counted. Uncare is the default blind spot, while abandonment is a measured and intentional withdrawal, a “selective forsaking” made after the sovereign weighs costs and benefits. At the end of this chain lies what I call the dead: not biological death (and whether one is biologically alive no longer matters), but the complete revocation of social recognition. A person still breathes and walks, but their existence no longer affects any decision and no longer enters any narrative. They cease to be a statistical “population,” cease to be a “risk object” within governance, and no longer even qualify as a nuisance to be administered. They live, yet are no longer treated as the living. In other words, “the dead” is a condition of erasure within institutions, echoing Patterson’s notion of social death, but here pushed to an even more extreme form: being entirely removed from the structure itself. In a way, this condition mirrors a line from László Krasznahorkai’s The Melancholy of Resistance, flipped inside out. The original offers comfort to the deceased: “You already belonged to us; only your body belonged to death.” Here, it should be reversed: “You have long been dead; only your body still belongs to us.” Where the original line suggests spiritual continuity, in the context of sovereign withdrawal it exposes something else as: what matters is never the dead, but how the living use them to stabilize their own world.

A person who becomes dead is not without a history. They know they were once “someone,” with experiences, positions, relationships, once holding shape in others’ narratives. They once belonged to this thing called “the story.” Now they have fallen out of it. Walking down the street, they appear alive, but no one cares. Their life or death makes no difference to the system. Today, during the Human Geography Seminar, Noam Leshem’s work on uncaring offered a concrete case: the Rukban encampment on the Syrian–Jordanian border. Once home to sixty or seventy thousand civilians displaced by war, Rukban was a “policy no-man’s-land,” a fissure belonging to no sovereign. Syria refused them, Jordan sealed its border, international organizations were blocked, Western forces maintained military presence nearby yet refused engagement. Thus the residents of Rukban were pushed out of the sovereign logic altogether, not exclusion, not exception, but de-referencing. They no longer belonged to any category of obligation or naming.

Society mourns and remembers the dead, but the dead gain nothing from any ritual and are not revived by remembrance. Mourning serves the living, to relieve guilt, stabilize order, prevent future crises. In other words, the study and memorialization of death are acts through which the living govern themselves, ensuring that we, the temporarily “recognized,” do not slip too easily into the dead. But in Rukban, even mourning is impossible. Its people are not permitted to become “the dead,” because even the dead can be ritualized. They are neither killed nor governed; they are simply suspended, placed in the system’s blind zone. This is not bare life, for bare life is still captured and managed by the sovereign. The lives in Rukban occupy a space beyond withdrawal, a fall after bare life: life that is no longer even bare.

This is also why, in human geography, environmental humanities, and political ecology, people keep staring at wounds and dissecting decay. Some split open their own wounds, using numbness or pain as samples; some open others’ wounds, submerging the rotting organs in conceptual formalin; some turn them into theory, artwork, or data. Knowledge production is itself a way of processing the dead. Researchers, policymakers, artists, they differ in method, but the essence is the same. The dead do not speak, the world speaks by processing them, thereby securing itself. And in Rukban, every sovereign action enacts this procedural indifference: Jordan used cranes to drop supplies over the border to avoid “contact”; Russia and Syria built “humanitarian corridors” to pull returnees back under regime control; American special forces sat within visible distance but refused even a bottle of water; the UN convoy brought clipboards instead of food. These gestures jointly sustain a structure in which people are allowed to exist, but not allowed to enter any relation of responsibility. Beyond simple killing, they produce a state where death need not occur, because there is no subject worthy of death. Policies are carefully maintained, borders meticulously managed, only people are not. The system tends to its own architecture while lives are trapped in its seams.

Death is a drifting black fog. Religion can package it, academia can package it, human emotion can package it. Its fluidity is precisely what allows it to be renamed and repurposed. Structural dead is the coldest form of this fog, with an empty space left behind by sovereign withdrawal. Nietzsche’s well-known line about “balancing reason and passion” is, at heart, about how a subject maintains shape under collision. Pure reason impoverishes life while pure passion makes it collapse. Thus, I was thinking, when the world is nothing but fog (emptiness, meaninglessness, the ungraspable), those who pursue “essence” will find nothing and those who surrender to passion will shatter under its weight. A subject able to maintain form within pain must both feel and restrain feeling. Thus meaning is not a truth inside the fog, it is the packaging the subject chooses, and the responsibility they accept for that choice. Omar, a young man in Rukban, was once a medical student in a Syrian city, his life trajectory clear. War pushed him into the border’s fissure, stripping his institutional identity. In the desert he built a small pharmacy, using smuggled medicine to maintain a fragile order. When he could finally leave, the “home” he returned to was rubble. He pitched the same tent he had used in the camp in a cousin’s backyard. In a sense, he did not return, he simply relocated his suspended existence. He lives, but he is nowhere. “I want to go home” becomes both a personal longing and a collective fact of an entire population stripped out of place.

Which brings me back to the thoughts of the living. All mourning is the living paving roads for themselves, protecting the future selves who may one day be vulnerable. The living are not helping the weak, they are helping the selves they may become. Everyone who seems secure today may be pushed out of the boundary tomorrow. No one holds a permanent position, the living are all temporarily recognized. Thus each person must choose where their meaning sits, how they withstand the world, and how they avoid being removed from its map. Leshem studies the edges of care, and what I call the dead lies in the darkness beyond that edge. The edge of care is not a line but a slope, a gradual slide. Sovereign withdrawal is not sudden but composed of small decisions, silences, and refusals. The living must face, how to avoid falling in a world that excels at producing the dead, and how to accept that they themselves might become the dead at any moment.

So, the dead is not the fate of some distant other, it is one of the possibilities already written into all our futures, some were just pushed earlier.
