Climate Fault Lines and the Politics of Vulnerability

On 10 November 2025, LSE held a public lecture titled “Fault lines: the new political economy of a warming world”. The lecture was delivered by Helen V. Milner, Professor of Politics and International Affairs at Princeton University, and chaired by Liam F. Beiser-McGrath, Associate Professor in the Department of Social Policy.

Milner outlined the argument developed in her forthcoming book co-authored with Alexander F. Gazmararian. She presented the uneven spatial distribution of climate impacts, noting that projected warming will intensify heat exposure, reduce agricultural productivity, and increase health and labour risks in many regions south of approximately 35°N latitude. Regions north of this line may not experience comparable levels of immediate damage. This divergence is shaping political incentives. Support for mitigation and adaptation tends to rise in areas directly affected by extreme weather events, while regions with lower perceived exposure display slower policy movement. Milner emphasized that positions on climate action increasingly emerge from lived experience rather than ideological persuasion.

During the discussion, a question was raised about the role of international coordination. Milner suggested that while multilateral frameworks remain relevant, the pace of climate action is likely to be driven primarily by domestic political coalitions. She described this as a movement from local experience upward, rather than top-down agreement.

My attention turned to how climate change reshapes habitability. Climate impacts do not only alter environmental conditions as they also define whether a place can continue to support stable residence. For example, coastal flood protection is often deployed in accordance with land value and existing infrastructure. The result is uneven protection. When investment concentrates on central commercial zones, peripheral residential areas encounter earlier exposure to risk. Displacement does not take the form of immediate relocation but occurs gradually as insurance withdrawal, rising premiums, and reduced public maintenance accumulate.

A similar process appears in agricultural regions where insurers adjust coverage in response to increasing climate volatility. Some farmers become unable to maintain production due to cost burdens. This shift does not register as a single event but becomes visible in long-term changes in land use, labour patterns, and household decision-making. These examples indicate that the climate fault line is not only geographic. It also determines which environments remain viable and which move beyond the threshold of sustained habitation.

Milner’s lecture highlights a broad structural transformation: political action follows differentiated exposure. The further question concerns how exposure becomes recognized or rendered invisible in policy and planning. Habitability is not neutral. It depends on institutional support. When support recedes, places cease to remain livable. Understanding this connection allows climate politics to be examined not only in terms of national commitments, but also through the mechanisms that organize everyday living conditions.
