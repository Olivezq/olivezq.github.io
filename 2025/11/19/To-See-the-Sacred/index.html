November 19, 2025

To See the Sacred Through the Environment

A/N: The starting point of this piece was October 31, when I read Taylor (2016)’s article on the sacred and nature. A sentence suddenly came to me- “to interpret the divine through the environment”- and it has stayed in my mind ever since. During Reading Week I went to Edinburgh. In the church I felt a kind of heaviness and pressure that was hard to describe, as if the space beneath the high ceiling was not empty air, and what hovered above the prayer benches was not air either, but massive stones. Everything was so still. The delicate stained glass made me think of the blood-stained histories of the world. Beneath the sacred, I could hardly breathe.

After I returned, I kept reading related literature and was also thinking about the OLS we learned in EC2C3. When I thought of OLS’s linear-algebraic essence as projecting y onto the subspace spanned by the regressors, a certain image appeared: if light shines on an object from different directions, the shadow it casts on a two-dimensional plane will change in shape. A coefficient is not “the truth”, it is only the result produced by one particular direction of light.

This image unexpectedly connected with my experience in the church, that is about some heavy three-dimensional structure, whose full form I could not grasp, being pressed into a shadow by a beam of light (a stained-glass one, perhaps). I thought: this might be why the conclusions in religion-and-environment research look so contradictory. The writing probably began from these fragments. Looking back now, I think the revised title, “To View the Sacred Through the Environment”, fits better.

I. Where the Shadows Come From

The linear-algebraic essence of OLS is to project y onto the subspace spanned by the regressors X. In other words, what we obtain is not a clearer view of y itself, but the shadow y casts on that plane under a controlled beam of light. Change the angle, and the shadow deforms, the coefficients are rewritten accordingly. A coefficient is only the outcome corresponding to one direction of light.

Imagine a three-dimensional object illuminated from different angles, its shadow flattened onto a two-dimensional wall. We can only see the shadow, and we argue about the object’s “true” shape based on that.

In the church, I usually think about histories of war, persecution, colonization, and domination carried out in the name of the sacred. Above the prayer chairs there is something non-theological: that this space holds a vast three-dimensional structure, but what falls upon people is only its shadow.

Guth and colleagues (1995), using a national sample, find that conservative Christian eschatology, biblical literalism, and religious commitment are significantly negatively associated with support for environmental policy. Sherkat and Ellison (2007), using structuration theory, break religion into schemata and resources and produce a set of mutually pulling results. Greeley (1993) argues that what matters is the believer’s image of God rather than “being religious” as such.

These debates made one point increasingly clear. What we see is religion after projection, not religion itself.

If we treat religion-environment regressions as a set of shadows, the question is no longer which conclusion is correct, but rather: how were these shadows cast? Which dimensions were included in the model? Which experiences never qualify as dimensions to begin with?

Following these questions, I moved from OLS to sacred experience, imagination, and emotional structure, then into histories of race and colonization, and finally back to the method itself.

II. Examples: Three Shadows

If we use the environment to view the sacred, what exactly are we seeing?

Place a few studies side by side and their “religious effects” barely line up. This is not because religion itself transformed dramatically over thirty years.

In the framework of Guth et al. (1995), religion is effectively reduced to three components: eschatology (the end is near), biblical literalism, and religious commitment (intensity of participation). When these variables are entered into a regression with attitudes toward environmental policy, the results are highly uniform. Conservative theological dimensions show a stable negative effect across different samples (clergy, religious activists, political donors, the general public), especially eschatology, whose coefficient remains significant even after controlling for party identification and ideology. Looking only at these tables, it is easy to conclude that “conservative Christianity is detrimental to environmentalism.”

Sherkat and Ellison (2007) adopt a different angle. Drawing on Sewell’s structuration theory, they divide religion into two layers: schemata, the internal cultural scripts about the world, nature, and morality; and resources, including church organization, religious texts, and everyday participation. In their model, respondents within conservative Protestantism can simultaneously display two religious logics pointing in opposite directions. One is the theology of “humans are to govern nature,” reinforced by literalism, which lowers support for environmental policy, and the other is the stewardship ethic (“nature is God’s creation and ought to be protected”) which elevates environmental concern and certain pro-environmental behaviors. These two logics not only coexist but often appear within the same individual. The regression results look like a set of “mutually cancelling” coefficients, because the same religious tradition contains both negative and positive influences.

Greeley (1993) pushes the split one level deeper. What concerns him is “what God looks like” in the believer’s imagination. In his analysis, the variable of a “grace-giving God” absorbs part of the negative effects previously attributed to literalism or political conservatism. This is especially clear among Catholics, as respondents with high “Grace Scale” scores support environmental spending at levels comparable to, or even higher than, the nonreligious. This does not mean Catholics are more environmentalist, but rather that equating religion with “belief in God” or “Christian/non-Christian” is an extremely crude simplification. What determines direction is the believer’s narrative and imaginative structure. Whether God is a judge, an abstract authority, or a presence that continually gives grace through fire, water, bread, wine, and the natural world.

If these three studies are viewed together, a single object named “religion” appears as follows: under Guth et al. (1995)’s lighting, it casts a stable negative shadow; under Sherkat and Ellison (2007)’s lighting, the shadow splits into two, one leftward and one rightward; under Greeley (1993)’s lighting, part of the shadow flips, revealing a new direction governed by the image of God.

In every study we choose the dimensions for “religion.”

III. Multiple, Yet Still Gap-Filled, Shadows

The shadows in Section II all point to the same gap: the effects of religion do not stem only from the content of belief but also from sacred experience, imagination, and emotional structure, and none of which can easily appear directly in a survey.

In his reconstruction of American environmental thought, Taylor (2016) documents numerous moments in which nature is experienced as sacred. Muir (1997) wrote in a valley that “the whole world seems a church, and the mountains altars.” Leopold (1966) regarded land as “the community to which we belong.” Schweitzer (1936) took “reverence for the will-to-live” as the starting point of ethics. People have felt that “the world itself is speaking” in forests, and that they are only one node within a vast network of life in the wilderness. Nature is therefore being granted something like a sacramental status, a bearer of intrinsic value, an object that can be revered and mourned.

Yet on the same land there is another line. Taylor (2016) also notes that European settlers, shaped by Christianity and Enlightenment rationality, often entered the New World with a disenchanting gaze. Nature was “resource,” and land and Indigenous peoples alike were objects to be possessed and managed. Declines in biological and cultural diversity were outcomes of the combined effects of specific theological and political-economic structures.

Both lines marched under the banner of “Christianity,” and examples of both can be found across churches and historical moments. Thus, the “Christian-environment relationship” cannot possibly be captured by a single dummy variable.

If we shift our attention from experience to emotion, the gap becomes clearer. In an environmental philosophy class, Gottlieb (2003) asked students to state, one by one, how they felt about ecological crisis. Some said fear, some anger, and one young man said, “If I really start thinking about this, I’m not sure I can keep living the life I’m ‘supposed’ to live. If I start crying, I might not be able to stop.”

Gottlieb treats these reactions as an entry point into spiritual resources. Prayer, confession, collective celebration, mourning, these are not for reconciliation but for giving these emotions a container, enabling people to bear the weight of the information itself. If a person can only maintain daily life by avoidance, turning off the news, refusing to think about climate crisis, he will eventually slide into what Kierkegaard (1980) called “the feeling of having no feeling.” The problem has not disappeared, just the person has lost the capacity to respond. Here, “caring about the environment” is not only a cognitive issue but a question of whether one can feel pain. One function of religious practice is to provide an institutionalized way to confront fear, sorrow, and guilt so that one does not collapse beneath the information.

These elements can scarcely enter a regression model. Survey items ask “Do you support increasing environmental spending?” “Are you worried about climate change?” But real emotions and motivations are more complex. For example, in Guth et al. (1995)’s model, “apocalyptic fear” and a believer’s sorrow for God’s creation might both appear as “concern” in the data, but these two fears lead to opposite pathways, one says the world is ending anyway, so nothing matters; the other says that precisely because everything is fragile, protection is urgent.

From this angle, Greeley (1993)’s inclusion of the “image of God” variable is effectively a small opening in an otherwise closed model for sacred experience and emotional structure. He finds that a grace-giving God is associated with greater support for environmental spending because such an image makes the world feel like a gift, an extension of grace, something that can be thanked, cherished, and mourned.

Once these layers are visible, the contradictory regression results seem not so mysterious anymore. Religion internally also contains multiple logics that conflict with each other, it holds sacred experience and disenchanting structures, mourning and avoidance, images of nature as gift and images of nature as waste site.

IV. Who is Allowed to Cast a Shadow

There is another set of questions that is harder to handle: even if we are willing to acknowledge the internal complexity of religion, and willing to bring sacred experience, emotion, and imagination into the discussion, whose religious experience is taken as the “standard”?

In American sociology of religion, many models implicitly use “white Protestantism” as the reference structure. Other traditions, such as Black churches, Indigenous religions, immigrant communities, are often compressed into very crude dummy variables.

In the Black church tradition, religious experience and environmental experience are tightly entangled. Religious language carries the memory of slavery and racial violence. Nature is often imagined as refuge, a space of escape from oppression, rather than as a frontier for development. For many Black communities, “the environment” is first a threat to survival, not a moral issue. Religion here may function to embed this threat into collective history, offering a vocabulary of interpretation and resistance. When this entire set of complex experiences is compressed into a variable like “Black Protestant = 1,” the resulting patterns will of course look “messy.”

In Indigenous contexts, the issue is even more extreme. Early European settlers viewed Indigenous peoples and nature together as “uncivilized others.” When discussing colonial narratives, Tuck and Yang (2012) use the phrase “deathlike monsters”: the enslaved and displaced become monstrous in the colonizer’s imagination, making violence easier to justify. I once thought of a comparison, that in American horror films the ghosts are often Black, while in Chinese horror films they are often women. Groups long subjected to exploitation, suppression, and control are shaped in mainstream imagination into “figures of terror,” something that must be contained or expelled. Indigenous peoples’ relationships with land have undergone similar “monster-making” in colonial narratives, their kinship-based understanding of land is described as backward, ignorant, irrational, so that resource extraction can proceed.

Regression models typically do not touch these imaginative structures when designing variables. Here, “non-identifiability” is not a technical problem but a problem of power. OLS requires “no perfect collinearity,” meaning that only variables that provide new information can become independent dimensions. But in the real world, power often decides before statistics who is allowed to possess “new information.” Some experiences are treated as worth distinguishing, while others are treated as just cultural variation. “The story you tell has already been counted by the dominant discourse as ‘the same thing,’ so no new dimension is needed to represent it.”

In this situation, talking about solidarity easily becomes empty. “Solidarity is an uneasy, reserved, and unsettled matter that neither reconciles present grievances nor forecloses future conflict (Tuck & Yang, 2012, p.3).” In environmental contexts this becomes even sharper. For different people, “the future” carries different meanings. It may refer to the continuation of kinship with land, or it may point more to emissions curves and scenic preservation.

V. To See the Sacred Through the Environment, Where the Shadows Come From

Circling back, the whole story returned to multiple regression. It requires us to select a set of regressors, construct a subspace, and then project y onto it. In religion-environment research, y may be “support for increasing environmental spending” or “concern about climate change,” while the regressors include religious tradition, strength of belief, image of God, political orientation, education, race, and so on. Technically, this is only minimizing squared residuals to obtain a set of coefficients. Epistemologically, it is a strong worldview as it assumes that the world can be decomposed into several dimensions, that these dimensions can be linearly combined, and that once we control the direction of the light, we can see a relatively stable shadow.

But in the field of religion and environment, each assumption continually exposes its boundaries. Discussions such as Lawhon & McCreary (2023) on post-development can add another layer: “sustainable development” is an abstract category that attempts to contain incompatible visions, and therefore repeatedly produces contradictions in practice. In contrast, whether in Catholic sacramental worldviews or in the “community of life” narratives of dark green religion, we find more concrete images: nature as gift, as part of God’s body, and humans as one segment of evolutionary history, bound to all other beings. These concrete images share one feature, that they describe a world that can be felt. One can feel guilt over the destruction of God’s world; feel sorrow over the tearing of the land community to which we belong; feel a near-religious sense of obligation when life on Earth is taken as sacred. Abstract concepts can persuade reason, but they rarely move the body. Concrete sacred images capture sensation, memory, and relation at the same time, making them far more likely to translate into motivation for action.

In this sense, “to see the sacred through the environment” has a double meaning: on one level, we are indeed using the issue of “the environment” to evaluate religion, whether it promotes action or obstructs it; on another level, the environment itself is becoming a way of viewing the sacred. When we ask who takes responsibility for this land, who mourns these polluted bodies, who treats nature as kin, as gift, or as resource, we are actually asking: what still qualifies as “sacred” in this era? Who has the ability and the authority to inscribe this sacredness into policy and collective imagination?

Returning to the OLS shadow metaphor, research does not restore a full three-dimensional object. What it gives us is a set of mutually incompatible shadows. The misalignment, tearing, and absence among these shadows are themselves forms of knowledge, because they show where experience cannot be linearly decomposed, where history has been folded, and where a disenchanted nature continues to persist in some stubborn form of sacredness.

Thus, beyond considering how religion influences environmental attitudes, we must also attend to that, when we use the environment to view the sacred, we are choosing a direction of light, acknowledging certain shadows, and allowing certain things to never enter the frame.
