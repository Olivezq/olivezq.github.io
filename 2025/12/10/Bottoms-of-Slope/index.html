Bottoms of the Slope, Forms of Building

On Law, Waste, and the Question of Building After Abandonment

Introduction

I remember two scenes from December.

In a seminar room, a lecture on Jerusalem: how Israeli settler organisations use Ottoman land law, British mandate procedures, Jordanian custodianship records, and contemporary Israeli property claims to remove Palestinian families from homes they have inhabited for generations. The violence is forensic and carried out through archives and judicial decisions rather than soldiers.

In a screening room, a documentary about waste pickers in Dakar: people who work in Mbeubeuss, one of West Africa’s largest dumpsites, building livelihoods from what the city discards. The state sees them as a problem to be modernised away. They see themselves as professionals.

At first these seemed unrelated. One takes place in a city saturated with competing sovereignties; the other in a space largely abandoned by governance. But as I returned to both, I began to see them as two responses to the same structural condition I have been tracing: what do people do when the system withdraws recognition?

I argues that neither “resistance” nor “survival” adequately captures what happens at the bottom of the administrative slope toward death. Instead, what emerges are different modes of world-making under constraint, each with its own possibilities and fragilities, and each revealing something about the conditions under which building remains possible at all.

Dereferencing Through Jurisdiction: The Case of Jerusalem

The lecture on Jerusalem’s Palestinian neighbourhoods—the Old City, Silwan, Sheikh Jarrah—offered an anatomy of dereferencing through jurisdiction: the use of legal procedure to render a population’s presence illegitimate while maintaining the appearance of due process.

After 1967, Israel redrew the boundaries of Jerusalem, expanding the municipal area from 6 square kilometres under Jordanian administration to 72. This was an ontological transformation. Palestinians who had not moved found themselves suddenly “inside Israel” and their land became confiscable. Their homes thus became claimable and their presence became, through legal interpretation, an error to be corrected.

The mechanisms are interlocking. The 1950 Absentees’ Property Law defines anyone outside the municipal boundary on a designated date as “absent”, even if they never left Jerusalem and the boundary was drawn around them retroactively. The 1970 Legal and Administrative Matters Law permits Jews to reclaim property in East Jerusalem owned before 1948, while Palestinians cannot reclaim property lost in West Jerusalem under the same principle.

Settler organisations like Elad and Ateret Cohanim operationalise these fragments into a dispossession machine. They locate Ottoman-era records indicating a plot was once part of a Jewish religious endowment. They petition Israeli courts to be appointed trustees of that endowment. They use that trusteeship to file eviction lawsuits against every Palestinian family residing on the land. The courts, operating within Israeli law, then predominantly rule in their favour.

The Palestinian families thus do not even need soldiers to be expelled. A family that has lived in a house for four generations just needs to be told that, according to a document from 1880, they do not exist as rightful occupants. And so their presence becomes a legal error.

This is dereferencing, but of a particular kind. The system does not simply ignore these families but sees them, names them, and uses its own rules to demonstrate they should not be there. They are legible enough to be erased and visible enough to be removed. Unlike the residents of Rukban, who were placed entirely outside any sovereign’s framework of responsibility, the Palestinians of Sheikh Jarrah remain inside a system. But that system is designed to process their elimination.

And yet they remain, appeal, document, testify and hire lawyers who know the game is structurally rigged but play it nonetheless. Because their claim is to specific land, specific homes, places dense with generations of memory. To abandon the legal fight would be to cede the ground itself, since international law and human rights frameworks remain the only arenas where their claim can be articulated in terms the world recognises. To refuse these tools entirely would be to become invisible in a different way.

This, then, is one response to structural abandonment: refuse to be dereferenced, even when the reference itself has become a weapon.

Building from Discard: The Case of Dakar

The dumpsite receives Dakar’s waste, and within it, thousands of people have constructed an economy. They sort and sell materials with expertise that formal waste management systems cannot replicate. They have developed knowledge illegible to official categories, such as distinguishing plastics by touch, smell, and combustion pattern. They have established governance structures such as territorial divisions, hierarchies, rules governing who works where and when. They have, in effect, built a world.

The state views them as a developmental problem. Official policy speaks of “modernisation,” of replacing informal waste work with mechanised systems, of “retraining” workers for “better” employment. The waste pickers refuse and they insist that this is a profession.

When filmmakers inquired about health(the obvious question), given that these workers operate in toxic conditions, the workers deflected. This is because if they are sick, the dumpsite can be closed “for their protection”; If they are fragile, they can be “saved”, which means displaced and dereferenced under the banner of care. Illness has become an instrument of dispossession. So they refuse vulnerability. They insist on being recognised as workers and experts, people who have built something rather than people who have only survived what was done to them.

This represents a fundamentally different orientation than Jerusalem. There, people fight to remain inside the system’s grammar—contesting documents, claiming rights. Here, people have largely exited that grammar. They do not petition the state to acknowledge their expertise and do not request inclusion. The dumpsite does not act as a waiting room.

One of the workers observed: “The dump is a mirror of Dakar.” Everything the city consumes eventually arrives. The waste pickers read the city through its refuse, seeing consumption patterns and the material unconscious of urban life. They interpret its secrets through its garbage. The waste pickers then produce value from waste. The city produces surplus populations; surplus populations produce a commons from what is discarded.

This is another response to structural abandonment: stop fighting for recognition and construct a world that does not require it.

The Limits of “Resistance”

Is this resistance?

The Palestinian families in Sheikh Jarrah are resisting in an obvious sense as they refuse to leave and contest every ruling. But their resistance is framed resistance. They must speak in the language of Israeli property law to be heard at all. Although they have few alternatives, it still reveals resistance’s constraints to some extent. You can only resist within the terms the system makes available to you.

The waste pickers of Dakar resist in a less obvious sense. They do not frame their daily work as political opposition and they are just simply living and building. But their refusal to be categorised as victims, their insistence on expertise, their construction of governance, these constitute what might be called practical refusal.

Yet here is what I keep returning to that neither form of resistance is freely chosen, and both remain structurally precarious. And crucially both can be destroyed. This leads to a question: Is there a condition under which resistance becomes impossible?

Return to Rukban, the encampment on the Syria-Jordan border that anchored my first reflection. The residents there were not fighting in courts as there was no court that would hear them. They were not building alternative economies as there was nothing to build with. They were placed entirely outside any sovereign’s framework of responsibility and they were dereferenced completely.

What does resistance look like when there is no one to resist against? When no sovereign acknowledges you as existing within their field of obligation? Perhaps, in such conditions, resistance shrinks to its most minimal form: still being there. The body’s bare persistence as the last form of political presence.

Recognising its existence matters, because it marks the outer boundary of what we can call “agency” at all.

Repair and Its Directions

In my previous reflections, I traced how care slides into control. Care, in the hands of the state, operates as a technology for governing surplus life. Both Jerusalem and Dakar add another dimension: the question of repair.

The state’s version of repair is always reintegration. It seeks to bring you back into legibility and back into categories it can manage. For the Palestinian families, repair would mean accepting the court’s judgment, becoming a “resettled” population. For the waste pickers, repair would mean accepting retraining, abandoning the dumpsite, becoming a “formal” worker. In both cases, repair requires surrendering what you have built in exchange for a place inside the system that abandoned you.

But what if repair need not point backward toward reintegration?

The waste pickers can maintain social order without official sanction. The Palestinian families, too, practice a form of repair, as their presence is itself a form of making, of a world that refuses to disappear even as official documents declare it already gone.

Repair, then, can also constitute resistance: repairing the world you have, rather than accepting the world they offer.

But is such repair sustainable?

The waste pickers’ world exists because the state has not yet decided to destroy it. The moment Mbeubeuss becomes valuable real estate, or the moment a new government prioritises “modernisation,” their world can be erased. The Palestinian families’ persistence depends on the slow grinding of legal procedure and a single decisive ruling could end it overnight.

Building at the bottom of the slope is always building on borrowed time. The structures are real but their foundation is the system’s temporary inattention. This is not to dismiss what is built though. This is providing a reason to understand its conditions.

What Remains

I began this series of reflections with a claim: the dead is not the fate of some distant other; it is one of the possibilities already written into all our futures. My second reflection extended this: we are all on the slope; some are simply pushed earlier.

These claims remain true, but they are not sufficient for the third reflection. The new question is not whether we will fall(structurally, many of us will), but what exists at the bottom, and whether it can last.

What I have seen in Jerusalem and Dakar suggests that the bottom is not empty. People land there and build. They fight within systems designed to exclude them, or they construct alternatives outside those systems entirely. They repair what they can with what they have and refuse to disappear.

But I have also seen that everything built at the bottom remains precarious. The Palestinian homes can be demolished. The expertise can be declared obsolete. There is no stable ground beneath the slope, only structures people have made, held together by effort and circumstance, vulnerable to forces beyond their control.

This leads to a final question, one I cannot answer:

Is it possible to build something at the bottom that the system cannot destroy?

Or is everything constructed in the zone of abandonment necessarily temporary, real while it lasts, but always waiting for the next clearance, the next administrative decision that renders it void?

I do not know. The Palestinian families have persisted for decades and the waste pickers have built their economy over generations. These are not trivial durations but neither constitutes permanence. Both exist in the space between the system’s abandonment and its return, the gap where life becomes possible just because power has looked away.

Or perhaps the question is not whether such building can achieve permanence, but whether permanence is the right measure. Perhaps what matters is not that the structures last, but that they exist at all, that even in conditions of radical abandonment, people continue to make worlds, however fragile, however temporary.

This is neither hope nor despair that, at the bottom of the slope, there is building. Whether it will remain, or whether it can remain, depends on forces far beyond the builders themselves.

And perhaps that is what those of us still higher on the slope need to see, about what it looks like to build when you have already fallen and what it would take for that building to survive.
