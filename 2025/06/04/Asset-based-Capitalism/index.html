Key Takeaways from Factions of Asset-Based Capitalism

Key Points from Melinda Cooper’s Lecture

“BlackRock vs Blackstone? Factions of Asset-Based Capitalism”

1. Central Argument: Finance Capital Is Not a Monolith

  • Finance is often treated as a unified class interest, but in reality, it is fractured along institutional, legal, and political lines.
  • The most significant internal divide is between institutional asset managers (e.g., BlackRock) and private investment funds (e.g., Blackstone).

2. Historical Foundation: The New Deal Regulatory Divide

  • U.S. financial legislation from the 1930s–40s created two distinct legal categories:

    • Public investment entities (mutual funds, pension plans) operating under tight regulations.
    • Private investment entities (private equity, hedge funds, venture capital) serving small groups of wealthy investors under lighter oversight.

  • This division was designed to protect the general public from high-risk, high-volatility financial products.

3. Business Model Divergence

  • Institutional investors are bound by restrictions on leverage, derivative use, and performance-based compensation.
  • Private funds are allowed to engage in high-risk, high-reward strategies and to charge performance fees known as carried interest, which are taxed at preferential rates.
  • These regulatory differences lead to distinct incentive structures and profit strategies.

4. The Concept of Conflicted Symbiosis

  • Despite their differences, public and private investment forms are economically interdependent.
  • Since the 1980s, institutional investors have increasingly supplied capital to private funds, a relationship enabled by regulatory changes such as Regulation D.
  • Cooper describes this interdependency as a “conflicted symbiosis”: the two forms of capital need one another but operate under separate regimes and often diverge politically.

5. Political Realignments in Asset-Based Capitalism

  • In the 1990s, mutual funds were aligned with Republicans, who supported privatizing Social Security through individual investment accounts.
  • By the 2010s and 2020s, institutional investors like BlackRock became more aligned with the Democratic Party, participating in green finance, industrial policy, and retirement reform agendas.
  • Meanwhile, private equity, hedge funds, and venture capital have increasingly moved toward the Republican right — particularly the Trump coalition — driven by opposition to capital gains taxation and regulation.

6. Taxation and the Rise of the Wealth Elite

  • Private fund general partners are compensated largely through carried interest, a share of capital gains that is taxed as investment income rather than salary.
  • This tax structure is a core driver of the hyper-concentration of wealth among fund managers and tech founders.
  • Most small investors in these funds see average returns; the disproportionate benefits go to top-level partners.

7. Capital Gains as the New Engine of Wealth

  • Since 1980, capital gains have grown from under 5% to nearly 40% of national income.
  • Today’s asset-based elites — private equity partners, venture capitalists, and founders — derive the bulk of their wealth from unrealized capital gains.
  • Much of this wealth is structured to avoid taxation altogether, often passed down through inheritance.

8. Policy Case Study: The Universal 401(k)

  • Once a Republican idea, the universal 401(k) — a government-backed individual retirement account — is now championed by Democrats and large asset managers like BlackRock.
  • This shift reveals how both political parties have moved rightward: Democrats now occupy policy positions formerly held by 1990s Republicans, while Republicans have broken with neoliberal norms entirely.

9. The Trump Coalition and Private Capital

  • Trump’s political machine is closely tied to private investment: private equity, fossil fuels, and controlling tech shareholders form the economic base of the MAGA right.
  • His cabinet and advisory teams include major figures from private capital, with hands-on involvement in shaping policy.
  • Venture capital — historically aligned with Democrats — shifted rightward during Biden’s term in response to proposed capital gains reforms and antitrust actions.

10. Beyond the U.S.: Global Implications

  • While the lecture focuses on the U.S., similar dynamics appear in the UK, Australia, and possibly the Global South.
  • Institutions like BlackRock adjust their strategies depending on national policy environments but consistently seek to expand asset-based markets through public-private arrangements.

11. Conclusion: Capital vs. Capital

  • The key political fault line today is not just capital vs labor, but capital vs capital.
  • Asset-based capitalism is internally divided along legal, tax, and ideological lines.
