Sustainability in a World of Inequality:Lessons from Sen, Okonjo-Iweala, and Stern

Youtube | Amartya Sen and Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala in conversation with Nick Stern | LSE Event
full details: Amartya Sen and Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala in conversation with Nick Stern: building sustainability in a turbulent world

Key Speakers:

Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala – Director-General, WTO

She assumed leadership at a time when the WTO was at its most fragile, facing rising nationalism, U.S. obstructionism, and the marginalisation of multilateralism. With her reputation as a skilled negotiator, she has aimed to revive trust in multilateral cooperation, shift WTO from stagnation to relevance, and ensure that the voices of developing countries, especially from Africa, are no longer sidelined.

Amartya Sen – Economist, Philosopher, Nobel Laureate

Sen’s life and research reflect a deep concern for the most vulnerable, having studied the structural causes of famines, inequality, and institutional failure. Though deeply embedded in elite academic institutions, his voice is one of radical empathy and global humanism. He critiques any form of reductionism, whether in identity, morality, or policy, and insists on nuanced, pluralistic perspectives.

Nicholas Stern – Economist, Chair of LSE’s Global School of Sustainability

Stern is a pragmatic optimist: he believes that the path to sustainability is not only necessary but also economically viable and desirable. His work often combines rigorous economic modelling with strong normative positions about intergenerational fairness and global equity.

Context

Climate Action Is Still Not Deeply Rooted in Policy

“We say climate change is an existential threat, and many young people feel that very deeply. But when you look at how policymakers are acting, it’s clear that this sense of urgency is not as deeply seated in their minds as it should be. There are a few countries and leaders who are better than others. But overall, we are not seeing the kind of whole-of-government approach that such a threat demands.”

Despite overwhelming evidence of climate change, policy responses remain fragmented. The idea of a whole-of-government approach is often missing. Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) are typically led by environment ministries without coordination with finance, energy, or trade ministries. There is a visible retreat from climate commitment:

  • Some private sector actors are pulling back from previous climate investments.
  • In some countries, discussing climate change is becoming politically taboo.
  • Even multilateral institutions are showing reduced engagement.

Climate Justice and Responsibility

Developed nations industrialized using carbon-intensive methods, creating the emissions stock responsible for current global warming. Meanwhile, the poorest countries, which contributed least, are suffering most, yet are also being asked to contribute to solutions. This creates a justice gap: “Who caused the problem, and who pays to solve it?”

We must reframe climate action not as a “burden” but as an opportunity to support fair, inclusive development. Investing in the Global South is not charity, it’s about enabling new growth stories through renewable energy, local job creation and clean industrialization.

Justice is best understood by identifying injustice, as the denial of rights or entitlements. Climate justice, therefore, is about:

  • The right to development
  • The right to a life people have reason to value (echoing Sen’s capabilities approach)
  • Intergenerational fairness, that emissions today compromise the futures of the young and unborn

Okonjo-Iweala describes rural farmers in Nigeria who don’t use the term “climate change,” but feel its effects directly: “The rains are coming at the wrong time, staying too long, or too short. We don’t know when to plant or when to harvest.” This makes climate justice tangible since it’s about livelihoods, food, survival.

Finance and Structural Reforms

The $100 billion/year climate finance pledge made under the Paris Agreement has not been met. The Loss and Damage Fund, meant to compensate vulnerable countries, has only secured $700 million so far — vastly below the trillions needed. Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs) are under-capitalized and cannot meet demand alone.

Proposals for Financing Climate Action:

  1. End Harmful Subsidies

Globally, governments still spend over $2 trillion/year on distortionary subsidies:

  • $1.2 trillion in fossil fuel subsidies
  • $600+ billion in agriculture subsidies (often trade-distorting)
  • $300+ billion in inefficient water subsidies
  • $22 billion in harmful fishing subsidies

Even repurposing half of that $2 trillion would be transformational.

  1. Boost Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs)

MDBs can act as catalysts, using public capital to leverage private investment.

To do that, they need:

  • New capital injections from shareholder countries
  • Reforms to improve coordination and risk-sharing

Okonjo-Iweala praises MDBs for improving collaboration, but stresses the need for greater ambition.

  1. Leverage Private Sector Finance

Private capital is essential to scaling up from billions to trillions. MDBs must use their balance sheets and guarantees to de-risk investments in clean energy, especially in the Global South.

Green Growth

Okonjo-Iweala pushes back against the false binary of “growth vs. climate.” Clean development offers a better path than the dirty, extractive models of the past. For example, African farmers are struggling due to erratic rainfall patterns, they may not call it climate change, but it’s a direct existential threat. Without addressing this, agriculture, trade, and development all suffer.

Africa holds 60% of the world’s solar energy potential and vast reserves of critical minerals (lithium, cobalt, copper) needed for energy transitions. Vision: Combine clean energy + local mineral processing to:

  • Create green jobs
  • Drive value-added industrialization
  • Empower young populations

With a modest injection of tens of billions per year, MDBs could triple their lending capacity. The capital structure of MDBs (paid-in + callable capital) allows high leverage. Combined with private sector co-investment, this could unlock the trillions needed.

Q&A

Question 1:

Many development banks and aid processes still reflect colonial structures. How can we decolonise them?

Answer:

The idea of decolonisation is important, but it’s sometimes overused or used vaguely. Instead of focusing on terminology, the emphasis should be on dismantling real structural inequities and power imbalances. The goal should be to improve representation, accountability, and equitable decision-making, rather than just relabel institutions. Excessive focus on conceptual purity can sometimes hinder practical reform.

Question 2:

Given the rise of fossil fuel investments in parts of Africa (e.g. new refineries), what is the optimal strategy for Global South policymakers balancing development and climate?

Answer:

There is no “optimal” solution in a textbook sense; we live in a second-best world with trade-offs. Some fossil investments already exist, the key is not to build more, but instead invest immediately and aggressively in renewables, which are now cheaper and more sustainable. Countries in the Global South should be given more time to decarbonize, with longer net-zero timelines (e.g. 2060 vs 2050), while still starting the green transition now.

Question 3:

Critical minerals for the green transition are often located in Indigenous lands. How can we balance the global demand for these resources with local rights and wellbeing?

Answer:

Extraction of critical minerals must respect Indigenous rights, environmental sustainability, and local communities. Projects should use clean energy and be guided by inclusive planning, not extractivism. The transition to clean tech cannot repeat the historical injustices of resource exploitation.

Question 4:

Most emissions today come from autocracies. Is the global rise of authoritarianism a threat to climate progress?

Answer:

Emissions are not exclusively linked to regime type, many democracies are major emitters and face similar challenges. Some authoritarian states have taken strong climate action based on self-interest, not ideology. The better lens may be political economy, particularly the influence of fossil fuel interests, rather than democracy vs autocracy.

It’s not accurate to claim that only autocracies are the biggest emitters or obstructionists, many democracies also emit significantly and fail to take adequate action.

China was historically criticized for its emissions, but in response, it stepped up climate action:

  • It now invests heavily in renewables and clean tech manufacturing.
  • China’s non-fossil fuel electricity capacity has surpassed fossil fuel capacity.
  • China has pledged to peak emissions by 2030 and reach net-zero by 2060.

China’s rapid move into clean energy is driven not only by external pressure but also by self-interest, recognizing climate as a national strategic priority. Other authoritarian states (e.g. Russia, Saudi Arabia) may be more obstructionist, but this is more linked to their petrostate economies than their governance models.

Question 5:

In terms of climate finance, how should funds be divided between mitigation (prevention) and adaptation (response)?

Answer:

Ideally, more funds should go to mitigation, since prevention is more cost-effective long term. But adaptation is urgent and unavoidable; many communities are already suffering. In practice, many investments (e.g. mangroves, decentralized solar) serve both functions, the dichotomy is not always helpful.

Question 6:

What role does community trust and cohesion play in climate transition?

Answer:

Trust is built through trustworthiness, demonstrated through fairness, consistency, and inclusion. During the COVID-19 pandemic, events like vaccine apartheid weakened global trust. Climate action must be grounded in moral legitimacy and practical cooperation to restore credibility and build shared momentum.

Reflections

I found its central message that we must “stand up and stand out” particularly compelling. It captured a sense of moral urgency that went beyond advocacy. While some parts of the discussion were cautious, they were also remarkably honest.

In particular, the call to move beyond terminology, for example, to stop fixating on whether or not something counts as “decolonisation,” and instead ask whether equity is truly being achieved. Rather than chasing purity of language or identity, the speakers encouraged us to prioritize action and structural change.

This message resonates strongly with a recurring weakness in much of modern (especially Western) liberal discourse: the tendency to talk without consequence, to perform empathy instead of enacting solidarity. This very problem was powerfully illustrated in the recent controversy around Jiang Yurong’s Harvard graduation speech. Her speech triggered backlash because it felt detached from real political stakes. Many found it unsettling because she universalized suffering in a way that erased asymmetries of power, saying “If a girl cannot go to school for fear of harassment, it threatens my dignity,” while standing on a global platform as a highly privileged speaker, came across as morally vague and self-centering. The discomfort was not about her calling for kindness, it was about the emptiness behind it.

The lecture, by contrast, took a firmer stance. It warned against equivocation and urged a shift toward accountable, institution-facing reform, particularly in trade governance, finance, and climate action. Okonjo-Iweala was especially powerful in her rhetorical precision. Her background gave her the authority to speak not only as a negotiator but as someone who has moved between global institutions and African governments, making her presence a living argument for Global South agency. Meanwhile, though Amartya Sen’s contributions were more philosophical and sometimes elliptical, he still underscored the importance of reason, humility, and a moral imagination that stretches across time and geography. His intellectual agility, even at his advanced age, was deeply moving.

Altogether, the panel did more than raise questions: it demanded that we act. In a time when the international system is fracturing, the insistence on standing up and standing out, even imperfectly, might be the most radical gesture left.
