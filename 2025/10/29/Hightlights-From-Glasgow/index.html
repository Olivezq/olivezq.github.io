Highlights from the Glasgow–LSE Environmental Data Lecture

How can statistics, satellites, and social media together help us understand and protect the environment?

That was the big question at this event hosted by the LSE Department of Statistics, featuring Professor Claire Miller from the University of Glasgow, Dr Sefi Roth from LSE’s Department of Geography and Environment, and chaired by Professor Milan Vojnović.

Claire Miller opened with a global journey through lakes, rivers, and cities, showing how modern statistics can fuse different kinds of environmental data. Her team combines precise but sparse in-situ water samples with the wide-coverage satellite data from the European Space Agency’s MERIS instrument to predict lake water quality at a global scale. By building spatiotemporal models, they can map how chlorophyll (a key indicator of water health) changes across 1,000 lakes worldwide, with each pixel showing its uncertainty range.

The spotlight then moved to Gallant — Glasgow as a Living Lab Accelerating Novel Transformation, a project where statisticians, social scientists, and city planners work hand-in-hand. Here, Miller’s team goes beyond traditional environmental data, diving into photos, social media posts, and citizen-science apps to understand how people perceive their environment. Using image captioning and sentiment analysis, they analysed over 30,000 Flickr posts from Central Scotland (2019–2024). Photos tagged “fallen tree after storm” scored negative; sunny park scenes scored positive.

The goal was to see if social media can act as a real-time environmental sensor, even helping city councils detect events like floods or storm damage automatically.

Dr Sefi Roth brought an economist’s eye and a memorable experiment. In partnership with London’s Camden Council, his team installed indoor air-quality monitors in residents’ homes. Half of them could see their air data live and the other half couldn’t. The result was simply giving people information reduced their indoor pollution exposure by more than 30 percent. It’s a striking example of how data transparency itself can drive behavioural change.

Both speakers cautioned against the “big data trap.” Miller warned that low-quality or biased data can easily drown out carefully curated datasets. Roth pointed out that some governments have even placed pollution monitors strategically in cleaner areas to meet targets.

With satellite-launch costs plummeting, Miller expects a boom in environmental monitoring from private and public missions alike. New technology can even capture 3D slices through the atmosphere, adding depth to our understanding of pollution and climate change. Both speakers see huge promise in combining AI’s pattern-spotting power with statistical modelling’s interpretability, letting us move from monitoring nature to truly understanding and managing it.

This lecture also connects to our GY222 class reading of Fullerton and Kinnaman (1996) this week, “Household Responses to Pricing Garbage by the Bag.” Both explore how people respond to environmental incentives, whether through economic pricing or data transparency. Fullerton and Kinnaman showed that households adjusted their waste behaviour when faced with a “pay-as-you-throw” fee, while this discussion demonstrated how access to information can similarly influence environmental action.
