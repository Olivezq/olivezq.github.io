Corridors of Power and the Domestic Imaginary

A little side note: “The Hidden Alchemy of Biochar and Basalt” was published a few weeks ago on Remineralize the Earth. It’s my first piece since joining last month, after an editorial review. I’m grateful to be part of a science-writing team of researchers and industry practitioners.

During this week’s GY220 seminar, I found myself playing the politician. The task was to decide whether to support managing land with fire, alongside representatives of scientists and Indigenous communities. Dr. Mille wanted conflict. However, what emerged was a kind of choreography, as I spoke in the language of consensus, the scientists justified through data, and the locals responded with reasoned calm. The locals said, “We cooperate with scientists, so we know that burning is good.” And that single line, actually just extinguished the drama right away. There was nothing left to debate.

I realised then that politics often works not through persuasion, but through the domestication of disagreement. My role as politician was to translate, to blur the edges of conflict until the room resembled stability. When Dr. Mille asked, “What if the fire frightens people?” I answered, “We will try our best not to let you evacuate.” The room laughed. Yet beneath the humour, there was a logic, that, risk can be acknowledged but only abstractly, fear must be managed and never inhabited.

It was this scene that returned to me when I listened to Evelina Gambino’s lecture, two day later. She spoke of another kind of management, not of fire, but of infrastructure, and of how corridors, ports, and pipelines promise connection while producing estrangement. Her research followed the long, unfinished story of turning Georgia into a transit corridor, a dream suspended between post-Soviet hope and capitalist exhaustion.

Gambino described the corridor as both planetary and intimate. It is a vast logistical form that reaches across nations, yet also seeps into domestic life. “People make and unmake corridors,” she said, suggesting that the global is not imposed upon the local, but translated through it, and in that translation, distorted, naturalised, or undone.

To domesticate, in her sense, is not to tame nature but to render the political familiar, to make the foreign project make sense at home. The corridor is brought into people’s houses, and in doing so, people themselves become extensions of it. In Anaklia, families reconfigured their homes into guesthouses in anticipation of the port that never came. Furniture was replaced by narrow corridors and orchards became rental rooms. When the project failed, these spaces remained half-inhabited, half-abandoned, becoming residues of a promise. “People were left to exist in unfinished things,” she said.

In that description I recognised a symmetry with my classroom workshop. The politician, like the corridor, translates uncertainty into a managed vocabulary. Both depend on the same fiction that planning equals progress. The phrase I repeated in class “we are working on it” carries the same temporal suspension as the never-completed port. It postpones confrontation and builds a corridor of delay.

Gambino’s ethnography unsettles the distinction between infrastructure and life. The state does not merely build but domesticates. And those who live within its plans - women, migrants, families — sustain the structure with invisible labour, remittances, and endurance. When she recounted how Georgian women marched with empty suitcases, declaring “We are the infrastructure”, it made visible what is usually silent: that connectivity is not material but human, and that the corridor survives by consuming intimacy.

If my GY220 class simulated the governance of land, Gambino’s research revealed the governance of time. The corridor stretches anticipation indefinitely, replacing completion with continuity. To live in such a corridor, physical or political, is to inhabit the present as an unfinished project. The houses in Anaklia, like the policies in our classroom, exist in the same tense: we will, we are, we plan to.

Her talk also recalled something else from that class: the locals’ insistence on collaboration. In her refusal to be confined by the “Indigenous” role Dr. Mille had assigned, there was something profoundly political, an act of disobedience that mirrored Gambino’s appeal to “turn the corridor inside out.” Both gestures expose how easily structures of power rely on the expectation of disagreement, and how solidarity, however fragile, can interrupt the script.

In the end, what I took from both experiences is a kind of double vision, which, in a way, resonates with the title of her talk, “Double Crossing,” since someone at the event had wondered why she chose that name. Politics, whether of land or of logistics, is built on translation between languages, between scales, between what is promised and what is lived. Yet every translation also distorts, domesticates, absorbs. The corridor, like the politician, claims to connect. In truth, it filters, delays, and defines who can move through.

To think critically, perhaps, is to pause inside that corridor, to look at the seams that hold it together, the gaps where power travels.
