October 2, 2025

Memory, Discrimination, and How We Tell Stories

In certain public settings, people often revisit past struggles and traumas through acts of recollection and narration. Photographs, archives, and testimonies are laid out one by one, as if to let those moments breathe again. Such scenes, on the one hand, feel like a serious engagement with history; yet on the other hand, they faintly carry a sense of consumption. Especially when some non-Black participants take on the dual roles of “witness” and “spokesperson” in such contexts, it is worth wondering whether their narration truly meant to preserve memory, or whether it, perhaps unconsciously, deplete a pain that originally belongs to others.

Within these acts of retrospection, I often notice a striking contrast. Some accounts resemble a re-enactment: photographs, newspapers, and archives are arranged in sequence, allowing the audience to relive those moments. But when Black scholars speak, the atmosphere changes entirely. Their voices carry emotion, a kind of urgency impossible to ignore. They are not simply “remembering” but bringing long-neglected experiences back to the forefront. I then realized that the power of narrative does not lie in how many details are retold, but in whether that urgency can be conveyed.

There are also moments that feel delicate. When conversations touches on heavy themes, some people inevitably use laughter to ease the weight. Yet such laughter sounds more like a defense than simple humor. It creates a lighter atmosphere in the room, but also, invisibly, diverts attention, blurring the gravity that ought to be confronted. Perhaps laughter, in such moments, is a posture. A way for people to cope with unease and helplessness.

I often think about the question of violence. It is not merely a series of isolated incidents, but more like a tension long embedded in society, always present like gravity. People ask: when will we truly be free from it? I think the answer may not be “complete escape,” but rather finding a balance in opposition to it. Perhaps it is a slow process of repair, like mending cracks little by little, using time and persistence to counter structural harm. Such repair can sometimes erupt suddenly, just as violence sometimes breaks through the surface. What truly matters is how short-term actions and long-term accumulation can connect, so that momentary efforts are not interrupted by political shifts.

This slow repair is, of course, far from perfect. People may grow disappointed when short-term results fail to appear, or feel that everything remains stagnant. Someone once remarked that the same protest photographs are brought out every year for commemoration, yet reality seems to change very little. Such questioning carries weight because it reminds us that commemoration is both a call and a cycle. Images are invested with so much meaning, repeated again and again, until at some point they become more like ritual than an impetus for reality.

While reflecting on this, I also notice the layering of discrimination. A seemingly casual remark may simultaneously carry biases of region, race, gender, even family roles. Such words resemble a blunt knife, on the surface offhand, yet in fact stacking multiple logics of oppression one upon another. More troubling still is that such logics are often amplified in the interaction between environments and groups. People are categorized into a certain “persona,” which then reinforces the existing environment in return, forming a cycle that is difficult to break.

Thus I begin to doubt: so-called “commemoration,” if it remains only at the level of symbols and stories, risks slipping into hollowness. How can we avoid falling into this mode of “re-enactment”? Why is it that Black struggles are narrated again and again, yet structural transformation comes so slowly? Meanwhile, are the experiences of Asians and other minority groups overlooked, or even further marginalized? These questions may have no definite answers, but the very act of asking them is itself necessary and worthwhile.

Perhaps commemoration requires more than restatement and reminder. It requires a mechanism that can endure. It should allow short-term efforts the chance to accumulate into long-term change, so that each scattered repair can take root and not be so easily erased. Change is never instantaneous, but if we can shift the social field of gravity little by little, perhaps the future will gradually tilt toward a direction with less harm.

This reflection is respectfully dedicated to LSE Black History Month.
