Gambling Beyond Rationality:Reflections and Appeals

A little side note: I have updated the section on Sköld (2025) Climate Melancholia in Recent Readings on Environmental Studies. The update connects Sköld’s discussion of climate melancholia with Afroogh, Mostafavi, and Jiao’s (2024) proposal of Intelligent Environmental Empathy (IEE). Those interested in the intersection of climate affect, empathy, and governance may want to take a look.

In the EC2A3 reading list, one assigned text is Stetzka and Winter (2023), How rational is gambling? (Journal of Economic Surveys, 37(4), 1432–1488). I would like to use this opportunity to discuss gambling more broadly. What follows begins with a brief note on the article and then moves into reflections. I then end with a personal appeal.

Stetzka and Winter pose a stark puzzle: why do people gamble when expected returns are negative? Their survey organizes explanations along a spectrum of rationality. At one end lies full rationality, where utility maximization can, in principle, reconcile betting with negative returns via risk love or portfolio diversification. Moving inward, bounded rationality acknowledges heuristics, probability misperception, and cognitive limits. At the far end stands irrationality defined as loss of control, with problem gambling and addiction carrying severe personal and social costs. Their conclusion is measured yet unsettling. No single account suffices and across the population one can find instances of rational consumption, bounded choice, and destructive loss of control.

Reading this, I am struck by what the rationality spectrum reveals and what it risks obscuring. Framing gambling as preference or portfolio can produce analytic clarity, but it may also neutralize the phenomenon, making it appear as merely another choice under risk. There is a danger that tidy models render the lived horror less visible. Conversely, instruction in probabilities and strategies can perversely amplify overconfidence. Some learners may convert cautionary knowledge into a sense of mastery, reading warnings as empowerment.

A further tension arises from method. Much of the field is statistical and model-driven, indispensable for inference yet thin on narrative. When the voices of gamblers, families, and communities fall out of view, the literature can miss the moral texture of devastation, the way money, time, secrecy, and shame rewire daily life. Gambling may need to be reframed less as a risky decision and more as a consuming force whose rationalizations help sustain it.

This brings me to a plain appeal. Do not gamble at all. The most dangerous moment is not the loss but the win: a win can alter how rewards are sought, how expenses are imagined, how “making money” is mentally encoded. After that, it becomes difficult to return to ordinary temporalities of work and waiting. Do not believe in “small bets for fun.” Do not take on loans to cover or chase. Do not assume that a partial confession to family leaves a safe exit. For families and partners: leniency often enables relapse; intervention must be firm, consistent, and early. Talent, training, and statistical literacy do not confer immunity. Many who think they can “beat the odds” fall fastest precisely because the sensation of early success becomes the argument to continue. I could say much more, but the essential point is that, please take it seriously. Gambling is far more terrifying than most imagine.

I have seen countless cases, and I thank the connective power of the internet for deepening this realization. While no one in my own family has been trapped by it, I have witnessed its grip on people close to me, including several teachers and friends, which made the danger all the more vivid.

The value of Stetzka and Winter’s survey is that it maps the intellectual terrain with care. Yet to grasp gambling in its lived reality we must hold analytic distance together with moral clarity. Models help us see; stories compel us to act. For this reason, I would suggest that after reading the survey, one also turn to some of the many real-life gambling stories available on the internet, in order to feel more directly what the models cannot capture.
